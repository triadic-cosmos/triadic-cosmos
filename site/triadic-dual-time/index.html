Dual Time

The Conventional View of Time in Modern Physics

Introduction

Across the major branches of contemporary physics, time is treated as a single, global parameter that applies uniformly to all physical systems. Whether one studies the motion of celestial bodies, the evolution of quantum states, the propagation of electromagnetic fields, or the thermodynamic relaxation of matter, the underlying assumption remains unchanged: there exists one universal temporal axis, shared by all processes, and its progression is fundamental rather than emergent.

This chapter outlines the mainstream conception of time as it appears in classical mechanics, relativity, quantum theory, and statistical physics. The purpose is not to critique these frameworks, but to establish the conventional baseline from which the dual-time perspective will depart in later chapters.

Time as an External Parameter

In classical mechanics, time is introduced as an external and absolute parameter. Newtonian dynamics assumes a universal temporal flow, independent of the observer and unaffected by the physical systems under study. The equations of motion,

$$m \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} = F,$$

are written with respect to a time variable that is not influenced by the mass, structure, or internal state of the system. Time is simply the background against which change occurs.

This treatment is deeply ingrained in the mathematical formulation of classical physics. The temporal coordinate is not derived from deeper principles; it is assumed. It does not emerge from the dynamics of matter or fields. It is imposed as a primitive element of the theoretical architecture.

Time in Relativity

Relativity modifies the Newtonian picture by making simultaneity frame-dependent, yet it retains the idea of a smooth spacetime manifold equipped with a global temporal dimension. In special relativity, the time coordinate is part of the Minkowski metric,

ds2 =  − c2dt2 + dx2 + dy2 + dz2,

and although different observers disagree on the slicing of spacetime into spatial and temporal components, the manifold itself possesses a well-defined temporal structure.

General relativity extends this by allowing the geometry of spacetime to be dynamical, but the role of time remains fundamental. The Einstein field equations evolve the metric with respect to a global temporal parameter, and physical processes are still described as trajectories through a four-dimensional manifold with a coherent temporal ordering.

Time in Quantum Theory

Quantum mechanics also relies on a single external time variable. The Schrödinger equation,

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(t) = \hat{H} \psi(t)$$

assumes that the evolution of a quantum state is governed by a universal temporal coordinate. This time does not depend on the system’s internal complexity, interaction history, or structural organization. A hydrogen atom, a superconducting circuit, and a macroscopic interferometer all evolve with respect to the same temporal axis.

Even in quantum field theory, where fields replace particles and spacetime symmetries play a central role, the temporal coordinate remains globally defined. The vacuum, excitations, and interactions are all formulated within a single temporal framework.

Time in Statistical and Condensed Matter Physics

In statistical mechanics and condensed matter physics, time is again treated as a universal background parameter. Relaxation processes, dissipative dynamics, transport phenomena, and approach to equilibrium are all described with respect to a single temporal variable. The equations governing diffusion, viscosity, conductivity, and decoherence assume that time flows identically for all materials, regardless of their internal structure or complexity.

This assumption is rarely questioned. It is taken for granted that a fluid, a crystal, a polymer, and a plasma all share the same temporal axis. Their differences are encoded in material parameters, not in the nature of time itself.

The Consequences of Universality

The global and fundamental treatment of time has enabled enormous progress in physics. It allows theories to be combined, compared, and unified under a single temporal framework. It simplifies the mathematical structure of dynamical laws and provides a common language for describing change.

Yet this universality also imposes a constraint: by assuming that all systems evolve with respect to the same temporal variable, mainstream physics implicitly assumes that time is independent of material structure, interaction history, and internal organization. Time is treated as a property of the universe, not of the systems within it.

Motivation for a New Perspective

The central aim of this book is to examine whether this assumption is necessary. By exploring the possibility that time may be emergent rather than fundamental, and system-dependent rather than universal, we open the door to a different conceptual framework—one in which the temporal behavior of matter arises from its internal dynamics, closure properties, and interaction structure.

The chapters that follow will develop this idea systematically. We begin by identifying the limitations of the global-time assumption, then introduce the concept of dual time, and finally show how emergent temporal structure leads naturally to closure, recursion, and the broader architecture of triadic unification.

The Four Fundamental Interactions and the Global–Local Divide

Modern physics describes all known dynamical processes through four fundamental interactions: gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force, and the weak nuclear force. Although these interactions are traditionally presented as distinct but coequal components of the Standard Model and general relativity, their structural properties reveal a deeper dichotomy. Two of them operate through globally defined spacetime fields, while the other two arise from local, material-bound dynamics. This distinction provides a natural entry point for the dual-time framework developed in later chapters.

Gravity and Electromagnetism as Global Spacetime Interactions

Gravity and electromagnetism share a defining feature: both are mediated by fields that are defined over the entire spacetime manifold. Their equations of motion assume a global temporal coordinate, and their influence extends without intrinsic spatial or material boundaries.

Gravity is encoded in the geometry of spacetime itself. The Einstein field equations,

$$G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu},$$

describe how matter and energy shape the curvature of a four-dimensional manifold equipped with a global temporal dimension. The dynamical evolution of the metric is formulated with respect to this global time, and gravitational effects propagate through spacetime independently of the internal structure of material systems.

Electromagnetism exhibits a similar global character. Maxwell’s equations,

∇μFμν = μ0Jν,   ∇[αFβγ] = 0,

are defined on the same spacetime manifold and evolve with respect to the same temporal coordinate. The electromagnetic field is not confined to material domains; it permeates space and interacts with matter through a globally defined potential. Its dynamics are governed by spacetime symmetries rather than by the internal organization of the systems it acts upon.

Both gravity and electromagnetism therefore rely on a single, universal temporal axis. Their fields are global, their propagation is global, and their equations assume a temporal structure that is independent of material complexity.

Strong and Weak Nuclear Forces as Local Material Interactions

In contrast, the strong and weak nuclear forces are fundamentally local. They arise from the internal structure of matter and operate only within confined domains. Their range is limited, their effects depend on material composition, and their dynamics are inseparable from the organization of the systems they act upon.

The strong interaction, described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD), binds quarks into hadrons and hadrons into nuclei. Its dynamics are governed by color confinement and asymptotic freedom, phenomena that depend on the internal state of the material system. The QCD Lagrangian,

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QCD}} = -\frac{1}{4} G_{\mu\nu}^a G^{a\mu\nu} + \sum_f \bar{\psi}_f (i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m_f)\psi_f,$$

defines fields that do not exist independently of matter. The strong force does not propagate freely through spacetime; it is restricted to the interior of hadrons and nuclei. Its temporal behavior is therefore tied to the internal dynamics of material systems.

The weak interaction exhibits similar locality. It governs processes such as beta decay and neutrino scattering, and its range is limited by the mass of the W and Z bosons. The weak force does not generate long-range fields; it operates only within the material domain where the relevant particles exist. Its dynamics depend on the internal configuration of matter and cannot be separated from it.

Both nuclear forces therefore rely on a temporal structure that is determined by the material systems themselves. Their dynamics are local, their influence is confined, and their temporal behavior emerges from internal organization rather than from global spacetime geometry.

A Natural Basis for Dual Time

The global–local divide between the four fundamental interactions provides a structural motivation for introducing two distinct temporal scales. Gravity and electromagnetism evolve with respect to a global spacetime time, while the strong and weak interactions evolve with respect to a material time that emerges from internal dynamics.

This observation is not a reinterpretation of existing theory; it is a direct consequence of how the interactions are defined. The global forces require a universal temporal coordinate, while the local forces require a system-dependent temporal structure. Dual time therefore arises naturally from the architecture of fundamental physics.

In later chapters, this dichotomy will be formalized and extended. The global time associated with spacetime fields will be contrasted with the emergent material time associated with internal closure and recursive dynamics. The four fundamental interactions thus serve as the first indication that time may not be universal, and that a dual-time framework may be necessary to describe the full structure of physical reality.

Planck Time as the Quantum of Global Spacetime

Introduction

The notion of time in modern physics is typically treated as continuous, smooth, and differentiable. Yet at the smallest scales allowed by known physical principles, spacetime possesses a natural quantum: the Planck time. This quantity arises from the combination of gravity, quantum mechanics, and electromagnetism, and represents the smallest meaningful interval for processes governed by global spacetime dynamics.

In this chapter, we examine the role of Planck time as the fundamental temporal quantum for gravitational and electromagnetic interactions. This perspective provides a structural basis for distinguishing between global spacetime time and the material time associated with nuclear interactions, which will be developed in later chapters.

Definition of Planck Time

Planck time is defined as

$$t_{\mathrm{P}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^5}},$$

where G is Newton’s gravitational constant, c is the speed of light, and ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. It represents the time required for light to travel one Planck length,

$$\ell_{\mathrm{P}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^3}},$$

and is commonly interpreted as the smallest interval for which the classical notion of spacetime remains meaningful.

The appearance of G, c, and ℏ in this expression is significant. These constants encode the structure of gravity, electromagnetism, and quantum mechanics. Planck time therefore arises from the intersection of these global interactions and defines a natural temporal scale for their dynamics.

Planck Time and Global Spacetime Dynamics

Gravity and electromagnetism are mediated by fields defined over the entire spacetime manifold. Their propagation, curvature, and interactions rely on the geometry of spacetime and the structure of the electromagnetic field. At sufficiently small scales, these fields cannot be meaningfully described without reference to the Planck time.

In general relativity, the curvature of spacetime is governed by the Einstein field equations,

$$G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu},$$

which implicitly assume a smooth manifold. However, at intervals shorter than tP, quantum fluctuations of the metric become dominant, and the classical description breaks down. The Planck time therefore marks the boundary below which gravitational dynamics cannot be treated as continuous.

Electromagnetic fields exhibit a similar constraint. Maxwell’s equations assume differentiability of the field tensor Fμν, but this assumption fails at scales where quantum gravitational effects become significant. The Planck time thus represents the smallest interval for which electromagnetic propagation can be described within a classical spacetime background.

In both cases, the temporal quantum is global: it applies to the entire spacetime manifold and is independent of material composition or internal structure.

Discrete Temporal Steps in Global Interactions

Although mainstream physics typically treats time as continuous, the existence of a minimal interval suggests that global spacetime dynamics proceed in discrete temporal steps at the Planck scale. This does not imply that macroscopic time is discrete, but rather that the underlying spacetime substrate possesses a smallest meaningful temporal resolution.

Processes governed by gravity and electromagnetism cannot evolve on intervals shorter than tP. Any attempt to probe such scales leads to energies sufficient to create black holes or induce quantum gravitational fluctuations that destroy the classical notion of time.

Thus, global spacetime time is effectively quantized at the Planck scale. The continuum description is an emergent approximation valid at scales much larger than tP.

Contrast with Material Time

The nuclear interactions—the strong and weak forces—do not reference the Planck scale. Their dynamics are governed by internal material structure, confinement, and local interaction histories. The characteristic timescales of nuclear processes are determined by particle masses, coupling constants, and internal degrees of freedom, not by the geometry of spacetime.

For example, the characteristic timescale of strong interactions is set by the QCD scale,

$$\tau_{\mathrm{QCD}} \sim \frac{\hbar}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}},$$

which is many orders of magnitude larger than tP. Weak interactions operate on even longer timescales.

These material timescales are emergent: they arise from the internal organization of matter and do not depend on global spacetime geometry. They are not constrained by the Planck time and do not require a universal temporal quantum.

A Structural Basis for Dual Time

The existence of a minimal temporal quantum for global spacetime interactions provides a natural foundation for distinguishing between two temporal domains:

  • A global spacetime time, quantized at the Planck scale, governing gravity and electromagnetism.

  • A material time, emergent from internal dynamics, governing strong and weak nuclear interactions.

This dichotomy is not imposed externally; it arises directly from the structure of fundamental physics. The Planck time defines the smallest meaningful interval for global fields, while material systems possess their own internal temporal scales.

In later chapters, this distinction will be formalized and extended into the dual-time framework. The Planck time will serve as the quantum of global time, while material time will be shown to emerge from closure, recursion, and internal dynamical organization.

Material Time as Local, Closure-Based Temporal Structure

Introduction

In the conventional formulation of physics, time is treated as a single global parameter that applies uniformly to all systems. This assumption is appropriate for gravitational and electromagnetic interactions, which are mediated by fields defined over the entire spacetime manifold. However, nuclear interactions—the strong and weak forces—operate within confined material domains and depend on the internal organization of matter. Their dynamics suggest the existence of a different temporal structure: a local, material-bound time.

This chapter introduces the concept of material time, a temporal parameter that emerges from the internal closure of material systems. Material time is discrete, system-dependent, and constrained by both dynamical activity and structural complexity. It provides the foundation for the dual-time framework developed in later chapters.

Material Systems as Closures

Material systems possess internal degrees of freedom that interact in a closed manner. A nucleus, a hadron, or any composite particle exhibits internal dynamics that do not propagate freely through spacetime. These dynamics are governed by the strong and weak interactions, which operate only within finite domains.

We define a closure as a finite, self-contained material system whose internal interactions are confined to its own structure. Each closure possesses its own internal dynamical state, interaction history, and organizational complexity. Because matter in the universe is finite, the number of closures is likewise finite.

Closures overlap spatially and temporally within the global spacetime manifold, but their internal dynamics remain localized. This overlap creates a continuous global field of matter, yet each closure retains its own internal temporal behavior.

Material Time as Local Temporal Evolution

Material time is defined as the temporal parameter governing the internal evolution of a closure. Unlike global spacetime time, which is continuous and universal, material time is:

  • local: it applies only within a specific closure,

  • discrete: it advances in finite internal steps,

  • finite: its domain is bounded by the extent of the closure,

  • interaction-limited: its propagation between closures is constrained by the speed of light.

Material time does not exist independently of matter. It is an emergent property of internal organization and arises from the recursive dynamics of the closure.

Discreteness of Material Time

Because closures are finite and their internal interactions occur in discrete events, material time advances in discrete steps. These steps correspond to internal dynamical updates—changes in configuration, interaction outcomes, or state transitions.

The discreteness of material time follows from three structural facts:

  1. The number of closures in the universe is finite.

  2. Each closure has finite internal degrees of freedom.

  3. Internal interactions occur in discrete events governed by quantum dynamics.

Thus, material time cannot be continuous. It is inherently quantized, with each closure possessing its own temporal quantum.

Interaction Between Closures

Although material time is local, closures interact through fields and particle exchange. These interactions propagate through global spacetime and are therefore limited by the speed of light c. As a result:

  • closures influence each other only after a finite delay,

  • material-time updates cannot synchronize instantaneously,

  • the global field of matter exhibits a structured, causal lattice of local temporal quanta.

This interplay between local discreteness and global propagation creates a layered temporal structure: discrete material time embedded within continuous spacetime time.

The Quantum of Material Time

The temporal quantum of a closure—the size of its internal time step—is determined by two competing factors:

  • dynamical activity, modeled by the entropy or internal interaction rate,

  • structural complexity, modeled by the mass or internal damping of the system.

High dynamical activity increases the frequency of internal updates, reducing the size of the temporal quantum. High complexity increases damping, reducing update frequency and increasing the temporal quantum.

We therefore define the material-time quantum Δtmat as a function of dynamical and structural parameters:

Δtmat = f(Sint, Mint),

where Sint represents internal entropy or dynamical activity, and Mint represents internal mass or complexity.

This correlation ensures that material time is not arbitrary. It is determined by the internal physics of the closure.

Material Time as the Basis for Recursion

The discrete steps of material time form a natural recursion index for the internal evolution of a closure. Each step corresponds to a new internal state, updated according to the closure’s dynamics. This recursive structure will later be formalized as part of the triadic cycle, where material time provides the discrete index for recursive evolution.

Material time therefore serves as the bridge between local nuclear dynamics and the broader dual-time framework. It captures the internal temporal behavior of matter and provides the discrete foundation upon which recursive dynamics can be built.

Toward a Formal Definition of Dual Time

The distinction between global spacetime time and local material time is now clear:

  • Global time is continuous, universal, and quantized at the Planck scale.

  • Material time is discrete, local, and determined by internal dynamical and structural properties.

Dual time arises naturally from this dichotomy. In the next chapter, we will formalize this structure and show how global and material time interact to produce the full temporal architecture of physical reality.

Linking Material Time to Electromagnetic Time: The Role of Mass and the Higgs Field

Introduction

Material time was introduced as a local, discrete temporal structure emerging from the internal dynamics of finite material closures. Global spacetime time, by contrast, is continuous at macroscopic scales and quantized only at the Planck scale. Although these two temporal domains differ fundamentally, they cannot be independent. Material systems exist within spacetime, interact through electromagnetic fields, and possess mass generated by the Higgs field. These facts imply that material time must be linked to electromagnetic time in a structural and dynamical manner.

This chapter develops the connection between global electromagnetic time and local material time, and examines the role of the Higgs field in establishing the temporal behavior of matter.

Material Time Requires Electromagnetic Time

Material closures do not exist in isolation. Their internal dynamics occur within the global spacetime manifold, and their interactions with other closures are mediated primarily by electromagnetic fields. As a result:

  • material time cannot evolve independently of electromagnetic time,

  • internal updates within a closure must be embedded within global temporal ordering,

  • interactions between closures propagate through electromagnetic fields at finite speed.

Electromagnetic time therefore provides the global temporal scaffold within which material time operates. Material time is local and discrete, but its steps must be compatible with the causal structure imposed by electromagnetic propagation.

Causal Constraints from Electromagnetic Propagation

Interactions between closures propagate at the speed of light c. This imposes a strict causal limit on how material-time updates can influence other closures. If a closure undergoes an internal update at material time step n, the effect of this update can reach another closure only after a delay determined by electromagnetic propagation.

Thus, the evolution of material time is constrained by:

  • the finite speed of electromagnetic signals,

  • the geometry of spacetime,

  • the global temporal coordinate associated with electromagnetic fields.

Material time is therefore embedded within electromagnetic time. The two temporal domains are distinct but inseparable.

Mass, Inertia, and Internal Damping

Material time is discrete because closures possess internal damping that limits the rate of internal updates. This damping arises from mass, which is generated by the Higgs field. The Higgs mechanism gives particles inertia, and inertia determines how quickly internal configurations can change.

The relationship between mass and material time can be expressed qualitatively as:

Δtmat ∝ Mint,

where Mint is the effective internal mass or complexity of the closure. Higher mass increases damping, slows internal dynamics, and enlarges the material-time quantum.

This connection is not optional. Without mass, closures would have no internal damping, and material time would collapse into the global electromagnetic time. The existence of material time therefore depends on the Higgs field.

The Higgs Field as the Origin of Material Time

The Higgs field provides particles with mass through spontaneous symmetry breaking. This mass determines:

  • the inertia of internal degrees of freedom,

  • the rate at which internal interactions can occur,

  • the damping that limits recursive updates,

  • the size of the material-time quantum.

Thus, the Higgs field is the physical origin of material time. Without the Higgs field, matter would not possess the structural complexity or internal damping required for discrete temporal evolution.

Material time is therefore not an abstract construct. It is a direct consequence of the mass-generating mechanism of the Standard Model.

Dynamical Activity and Entropic Drive

While mass determines the damping of internal updates, dynamical activity determines their frequency. Internal entropy, interaction rates, and degrees of freedom contribute to the dynamical drive of the closure. High dynamical activity reduces the material-time quantum, while high mass increases it.

We therefore define the material-time quantum as a correlation between dynamical activity and structural complexity:

Δtmat = f(Sint, Mint),

where Sint represents internal entropy or interaction rate, and Mint represents mass or complexity.

This correlation ensures that material time is physically grounded and system-dependent.

Unified Temporal Structure

Material time and electromagnetic time form a unified temporal architecture:

  • Electromagnetic time provides the global temporal coordinate and causal structure.

  • Material time provides the local, discrete temporal evolution of closures.

  • Mass from the Higgs field determines the damping and discreteness of material time.

  • Electromagnetic propagation limits the interaction between closures.

Material time is therefore not an independent temporal domain. It is a local temporal structure embedded within global spacetime time, constrained by electromagnetic propagation, and generated by the mass-giving mechanism of the Higgs field.

Toward the Formal Definition of Dual Time

The structural relationship between electromagnetic time and material time now becomes clear:

  • Global time is continuous at macroscopic scales and quantized at the Planck scale.

  • Material time is discrete, local, and determined by mass and dynamical activity.

  • The two temporal domains are linked through causal propagation and internal damping.

Dual time arises naturally from this relationship. In the next chapter, we will formalize the dual-time framework and show how global and material time interact to produce the full temporal architecture of physical reality.

Formal Definition of Dual Time and Foundational Conjectures

Introduction

The preceding chapters introduced the structural motivation for distinguishing between two temporal domains: electromagnetic time, which governs the propagation of fields across the spacetime manifold, and material time, which governs the internal evolution of finite closures. We now formalize this distinction. The goal of this chapter is not to present a final theory of time, but to provide a mathematically explicit and empirically actionable framework that captures the essential behaviour of dual time.

The formalism presented here is a proposal. Other functional forms may be equally valid or even preferable in future refinements. What matters is the structural insight: matter and fields evolve on distinct but dynamically coupled clocks, and this layered temporal architecture resolves several long- standing anomalies in astrophysics, cosmology, and high-energy physics.

Electromagnetic Time

Electromagnetic time, denoted tEM, is the universal temporal parameter governing the propagation of photons, field quanta, and holographic information. It is closure-transversal: all closures share the same electromagnetic clock. As shown in the dual-time formalism , electromagnetic time possesses a fundamental resolution limit given by the Planck time,

$$t_{\mathrm{P}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^5}},$$

which represents the smallest meaningful interval of tEM for field propagation.

Electromagnetic time does not depend on closure-internal entropy, mass, or recursion index. It is global, continuous at macroscopic scales, and quantized only at the Planck scale.

Material Time

Material time, denoted tmat, is the internal temporal parameter of a closure. It governs the evolution of internal degrees of freedom, recursive dynamics, entropic production, and structural reorganization. Material time is:

  • local: each closure possesses its own material-time flow,

  • discrete: internal evolution proceeds in finite steps,

  • finite: bounded by the closure’s spatial and dynamical extent,

  • mass-damped: inertia slows internal recursion,

  • entropy-driven: dynamical activity accelerates internal recursion.

Material time is not constrained by the Planck limit. Its resolution is closure-dependent and may be smaller or larger than tP .

General Dual-Time Relation

To link the two temporal domains, we introduce a general dynamical relation between material time and electromagnetic time. The relation must satisfy several structural requirements:

  1. Entropy accelerates material time.

  2. Mass damps material time.

  3. Material time remains strictly positive for active closures.

  4. Electromagnetic time does not directly drive material-time acceleration.

  5. Closures synchronize through electromagnetic interaction.

  6. The relation must allow additive, multiplicative, or functional variants.

A general form satisfying these requirements is:

$$\frac{dt_{\mathrm{mat}}}{dt_{\mathrm{EM}}} = F\big(S, M_{\mathrm{eff}}, I, t_{\mathrm{mat}} - t_{\mathrm{EM}}\big),$$

where F is any function satisfying the structural constraints above.

A minimal additive proposal, consistent with the dual-time formalism , is:

$$\frac{dt_{\mathrm{mat}}}{dt_{\mathrm{EM}}} = 1 + \alpha S - \beta M_{\mathrm{eff}} - K(I)\big(t_{\mathrm{mat}} - t_{\mathrm{EM}}\big).$$

A multiplicative variant is equally valid:
