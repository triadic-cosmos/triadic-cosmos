Introduction

The Great Filter is traditionally interpreted as a catastrophic barrier that prevents civilisations from reaching interstellar maturity. Classical accounts emphasise contingent events—nuclear war, pandemics, ecological collapse, technological accidents, or cosmic hazards—and treat civilisational failure as a probabilistic outcome of historical misfortune. Within the Triadic Cosmos ecosystem, however, the Great Filter has been reinterpreted as an architectural bifurcation: a deterministic choice between expansionism and sustainability . Expansionism overshoots closure, accelerates entropy, and destabilises causal chains; sustainability maintains compatibility with the finite domain in which a civilisation exists.

This paper extends that architectural interpretation by introducing a geometric origin of the Great Filter. We show that the filter is activated not merely by expansionist behaviour within a planetary closure, but by the geometric fragmentation of closure itself. When a civilisation distributes its population, infrastructure, or strategic capacity across planetary distances, it introduces unavoidable asymmetries in detection, temporal synchronisation, launch dynamics, biocompatibility, cultural evolution, and strategic vulnerability. These asymmetries collapse MAD stability, generate pre-emptive incentives, and produce long-term destabilisation that no civilisation can correct.

The geometric argument developed here builds directly on the structural limits established in and . Closed ecosystems fail because they accumulate degradation, instability, and entropy; megastructures fail because they exceed the energetic, ecological, and dynamical capacity of any finite domain; closure-breaking attempts fail because they require access to extra-closure mechanisms that cannot be generated from within a closure. These results collectively show that civilisational stability is strictly local. The present paper demonstrates that even modest attempts to fragment closure—such as multi-planet colonisation within the Earth–Moon–Mars domain—constitute a geometric form of closure-breaking with identical structural consequences.

The central claim is therefore architectural: the Great Filter arises when a civilisation attempts to extend itself across geometric scales that its closure cannot support. Civilisations that remain geometrically coherent within a single closure retain stability; civilisations that fragment closure across planetary distances activate irreversible failure modes. This geometric origin of the Great Filter provides a unified explanation for civilisational confinement, the absence of interplanetary expansion, and the continued silence of the cosmos.

The MAD Paradigm within Local Closure

Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) is the classical stabilisation mechanism governing nuclear deterrence within a single planetary closure. MAD assumes that two conditions hold: (1) both actors possess symmetric destructive capacity, and (2) both actors possess symmetric detection, response, and decision capacity. Under these conditions, any first strike guarantees retaliation, making conflict irrational and stabilising the system through deterrence.

MAD is therefore not merely a political doctrine but a geometric and informational structure. It requires co-location within a shared closure: minimal spatial separation, negligible temporal delay, symmetric launch dynamics, and complete causal accessibility. Within such a domain, detection is immediate, response is synchronous, and escalation is bounded by shared physical constraints. MAD stability is thus an emergent property of closure itself.

The architectural analyses in and show that closure provides the necessary conditions for symmetric detection, symmetric vulnerability, and symmetric response. These conditions break down as soon as closure is fragmented across planetary distances. Even modest geometric separation introduces asymmetries in temporal synchronisation, detection latency, launch feasibility, and vulnerability profiles.

MAD therefore operates only within a single closure. It is not a universal stabilisation mechanism but a local one. Once geometric separation exceeds the causal and informational capacity of the closure, MAD collapses. The present paper shows that multi-planet expansion introduces precisely the geometric asymmetries that render MAD physically impossible. This collapse of MAD is the first structural step in the geometric origin of the Great Filter.

Local Closure and Geometric Stability

Local closure is the foundational architectural unit of the Triadic Cosmos. A closure is a horizon-bounded domain in which causal accessibility, energetic capacity, informational structure, and recursive stability cohere. Within such a domain, the triadic cycle E → G → I → E is well-defined, positive, holographically consistent, and dynamically stable. Civilisational stability is therefore not an emergent property of culture or technology, but a geometric consequence of confinement within a single closure.

The analyses in demonstrate that closed systems possess finite corrective capacity: ecological subsystems drift, mechanical structures degrade, autonomy collapses, and entropy accumulates. These failure modes reveal that closure is not merely a boundary but a stabilising geometry. A civilisation remains coherent only while its activity, infrastructure, and causal loops remain contained within a domain whose geometric scale matches its corrective capacity.

Local closure also imposes symmetry. Within a single planetary domain, actors share identical physical constraints: identical detection horizons, identical temporal synchronisation, identical launch dynamics, and identical vulnerability profiles. This symmetry is not political but geometric. It arises because all actors inhabit the same causal basin, the same energetic budget, and the same informational horizon. MAD stability, as shown in the previous section, is an emergent consequence of this symmetry.

Geometric stability therefore requires co-location. When all actors remain within a single closure, causal loops are short, detection is immediate, response is synchronous, and escalation is bounded. The closure acts as a geometric equaliser: it enforces symmetry through shared physical constraints. Civilisational stability is thus a local phenomenon, maintained by the geometric coherence of the domain.

The present paper shows that this stability collapses as soon as closure is fragmented. Multi-planet expansion introduces geometric separation that exceeds the causal, energetic, and informational capacity of the closure. Even modest planetary distances—such as those between Earth, the Moon, and Mars—produce structural asymmetries that cannot be corrected. These asymmetries destabilise MAD, break symmetry, and activate the geometric origin of the Great Filter.

Local closure is therefore not merely a boundary of physical law; it is the geometric condition for civilisational stability. Fragmenting closure across planetary scales transforms a stable domain into an architecturally unstable one. Geometric Asymmetries in Multi-Planet Expansion

Multi-planet expansion fragments a civilisation across geometric scales that exceed the causal, informational, and energetic capacity of its local closure. Even within the modest spatial separation of the Earth–Moon–Mars domain, geometric distance introduces structural asymmetries that cannot be corrected by technology, governance, or intelligence. These asymmetries destabilise MAD, break closure symmetry, and activate the architectural mechanisms underlying the Great Filter.

We analyse six geometric asymmetries that arise inevitably from closure fragmentation.

Detection Latency and Horizon Discontinuity

Detection is immediate within a single closure: causal signals propagate across short distances, temporal synchronisation is trivial, and surveillance horizons overlap. Planetary separation breaks this symmetry. Electromagnetic latency between Earth and Mars ranges from several minutes to over twenty minutes depending on orbital geometry. This delay is structurally incompatible with MAD, which requires synchronous detection and instantaneous response. Horizon discontinuity further prevents continuous monitoring of launch sites, mass drivers, and underground facilities. Detection becomes probabilistic rather than guaranteed, rendering retaliation uncertain and destabilising deterrence.

Temporal Desynchronisation

Local closure enforces shared temporal flow. Planetary separation introduces differential temporal regimes: communication delay, asynchronous decision cycles, and non-overlapping windows for crisis response. These desynchronisations create decision gaps in which pre-emptive action becomes rational. A civilisation distributed across planets cannot maintain the synchronous temporal structure required for MAD stability.

Asymmetric Launch Dynamics

Launch dynamics are symmetric within a single planetary domain. Across planets, they become structurally asymmetric. Mars possesses lower gravity, thinner atmosphere, and lower escape velocity, making high-mass kinetic launch systems—railguns, mass drivers, and electromagnetic accelerators—far more feasible than on Earth. This asymmetry creates a geometric advantage that cannot be neutralised. A low-tech Mars colony can launch high-energy projectiles that Earth cannot intercept or match. MAD requires symmetric destructive capacity; planetary geometry guarantees asymmetry.

Kinetic Vulnerability Asymmetry

Earth is densely populated, ecologically fragile, and gravitationally deep. Mars is sparse, modular, and composed of distributed habitats. A single kinetic impactor can devastate Earth’s biosphere; the same impact on Mars damages only local infrastructure. This asymmetry in vulnerability creates incentives for pre-emption and destabilises deterrence. MAD requires symmetric vulnerability; planetary geometry guarantees asymmetry.

Biocompatibility Divergence

Over generational timescales, planetary separation produces biological divergence. Humans born and raised in 0.38g develop distinct musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, and vestibular profiles. Earth-born humans cannot function on Mars without extensive adaptation; Mars-born humans cannot safely return to Earth. This divergence creates functional incompatibility between populations, amplifying cultural separation and reducing shared identity. MAD requires shared identity and mutual comprehension; planetary geometry erodes both.

Cultural and Strategic Divergence

Geometric separation produces cultural divergence. Mars-based populations develop frontier psychology, high-risk tolerance, and underdog identity. Earth-based populations develop paternalistic attitudes and centralised governance. Divergent cultures produce divergent escalation norms, divergent risk perception, and divergent strategic logic. MAD requires shared norms; planetary geometry guarantees divergence.

Conclusion: Geometric Asymmetry as Structural Instability

These asymmetries are not technological limitations but geometric consequences of closure fragmentation. They arise inevitably from planetary separation and cannot be corrected by communication infrastructure, surveillance systems, governance models, or artificial intelligence. Multi-planet expansion therefore transforms a stable closure into an architecturally unstable domain. Collapse of MAD under Closure Fragmentation

The geometric asymmetries introduced by multi-planet expansion destabilise the structural conditions required for Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD). As shown in Section 2, MAD is not a political doctrine but a geometric stabilisation mechanism that emerges only within a single closure. It requires symmetric detection, symmetric temporal flow, symmetric launch dynamics, and symmetric vulnerability. Closure fragmentation breaks each of these symmetries. The result is not a weakened form of MAD but the complete collapse of the MAD paradigm.

We analyse the collapse of MAD across four architectural dimensions.

Loss of Symmetric Detection

MAD requires immediate and symmetric detection of any launch event. Planetary separation introduces detection latency, horizon discontinuity, and probabilistic surveillance. A civilisation distributed across Earth, the Moon, and Mars cannot maintain continuous monitoring of mass drivers, underground facilities, or orbital launch platforms. Detection becomes delayed, incomplete, and asymmetric. Retaliation becomes uncertain. Deterrence fails.

Loss of Symmetric Temporal Flow

MAD requires synchronous temporal flow: identical decision windows, identical escalation timelines, and identical crisis-response horizons. Planetary separation introduces differential temporal regimes. Communication delay produces decision gaps in which pre-emptive action becomes rational. Asynchronous escalation cycles create windows in which one actor can act while the other cannot respond. Temporal symmetry collapses. MAD becomes physically impossible.

Loss of Symmetric Launch Dynamics

MAD requires symmetric destructive capacity. Planetary geometry guarantees asymmetry. Mars possesses lower gravity, thinner atmosphere, and lower escape velocity, making high-mass kinetic launch systems far more feasible than on Earth. A low-tech Mars colony can launch projectiles with nuclear-equivalent kinetic energy that Earth cannot intercept. Earth cannot match this capability. Launch symmetry collapses. MAD becomes strategically incoherent.

Loss of Symmetric Vulnerability

MAD requires symmetric vulnerability: both actors must face comparable consequences from a first strike. Planetary geometry guarantees asymmetry. Earth is densely populated and ecologically fragile; Mars is sparse, modular, and composed of distributed habitats. A single kinetic impactor can devastate Earth’s biosphere; the same impact on Mars damages only local infrastructure. Vulnerability symmetry collapses. MAD becomes strategically unstable.

Architectural Consequence: MAD Cannot Survive Closure Fragmentation

These four collapses are not technological failures but geometric consequences of closure fragmentation. They arise inevitably from planetary separation and cannot be corrected by surveillance infrastructure, communication networks, governance models, or artificial intelligence. MAD is a closure-bound mechanism. It cannot operate across planetary distances.

The collapse of MAD is therefore the first structural step in the geometric origin of the Great Filter. Once MAD fails, deterrence fails. Once deterrence fails, pre-emptive logic emerges. Sci-Fi Deterrence Systems and the Illusion of Interplanetary Stability

A common response to the geometric instability introduced by closure fragmentation is the science-fiction proposal that civilisations could deploy interplanetary deterrence vessels: large, continuously manned spacecraft equipped with nuclear or kinetic weapons, operating analogously to terrestrial nuclear submarines. Such vessels are imagined as mobile stabilisers capable of restoring MAD symmetry across planetary distances. This section demonstrates that such systems are architecturally impossible and cannot resolve the geometric collapse of MAD.

Continuous Operation as Structural Impossibility

Nuclear submarines operate within a planetary closure that provides continuous resupply, maintenance, crew rotation, ecological buffering, and industrial support. Their stability depends entirely on the surrounding closure. An interplanetary deterrence vessel would lack all such support. As shown in , closed systems accumulate ecological drift, mechanical degradation, autonomy failure, and entropy. A spacecraft intended for continuous multi-year or multi-decade operation would be a closed ecosystem, and therefore structurally unstable. Even with advanced technology, continuous manning is architecturally infeasible.

Maintenance and Repair Beyond Closure Capacity

Nuclear submarines rely on extensive maintenance cycles, dry-dock overhauls, and industrial supply chains. An interplanetary deterrence vessel would require equivalent or greater support. No planetary system can sustain the industrial throughput required to maintain such vessels across planetary distances. As shown in , closure-breaking mechanisms cannot be generated from within closure. A deterrence vessel is therefore not merely difficult to maintain; it is architecturally impossible to sustain.

Autonomy Instability and Command Failure

Long-duration autonomous systems degrade over time. Deep-space probes demonstrate sensor drift, radiation damage, software error accumulation, and attitude-control failure. A deterrence vessel equipped with nuclear or high-energy kinetic weapons cannot rely on autonomy without catastrophic risk. Continuous human presence is equally infeasible due to ecological and psychological instability. Command reliability collapses under closure fragmentation. A deterrence system that cannot guarantee control becomes destabilising rather than stabilising.

Geometric Limitations of Interplanetary Deterrence

Even if such vessels were physically sustainable—which they are not—they would not restore MAD symmetry. Planetary geometry imposes unavoidable constraints:

  • detection latency prevents synchronous response,

  • temporal desynchronisation creates decision gaps,

  • launch asymmetry between Earth and Mars remains unchanged,

  • vulnerability asymmetry remains structurally embedded,

  • cultural divergence continues to amplify escalation risk.

A deterrence vessel cannot eliminate geometric asymmetry. It cannot restore closure symmetry. It cannot stabilise MAD across planetary distances.

Architectural Consequence

Sci-fi deterrence systems are therefore not viable stabilisers but closure-breaking fantasies. They presuppose continuous operation, perfect autonomy, infinite maintenance capacity, and geometric symmetry that cannot exist outside a single closure. They fail for the same structural reasons as closed-ecosystem starships, megastructures, warp drives, and interstellar travel. They cannot prevent the collapse of MAD under closure fragmentation.

Interplanetary deterrence is not a solution to geometric instability; it is a manifestation of the same architectural impossibility. The Great Filter remains a geometric constraint that no sci-fi stabilisation mechanism can overcome.

Ecological and Systems-Theoretic Collapse Models

Ecological collapse models emphasise that closed systems accumulate instability, drift, and entropy.
