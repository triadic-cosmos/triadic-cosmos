Introduction

Periods of rapid technological change are frequently accompanied by inflationary phenomena.

Historically, these inflationary episodes have often been discussed through the language of finance. Asset prices rise rapidly, valuations expand, expectations become increasingly optimistic, and observers begin debating whether a speculative bubble is forming.

Such discussions remain important.

However, recent developments surrounding artificial intelligence suggest that inflationary dynamics may emerge in forms that extend beyond traditional monetary or valuation-centric interpretations.

Modern AI systems increasingly demonstrate the ability to produce large amounts of software, scientific analysis, vulnerability reports, mathematical proofs, research hypotheses, and other cognitive artifacts. At the same time, unprecedented quantities of capital are being directed toward AI infrastructure, datacenter construction, model development, and associated ecosystems.

These developments are often discussed as separate phenomena.

The present paper argues that they may instead represent different manifestations of a common underlying mechanism.

Within the Triadic Cosmos ecosystem, intelligence-related systems are frequently described through interacting triads coupled by recursive feedback processes. Previous work introduced the Closure Overshoot Law, which describes how recursive closures may temporarily exceed their own carrying capacity, and Temporal Misalignment, which describes how structurally coupled subsystems often evolve at incompatible rates, creating persistent forms of imbalance.

This paper extends these ideas through the introduction of Triadic Inflation.

The central claim is that inflation need not be restricted to money, asset prices, or financial value. More generally, inflation can be understood as a reduction in the relative value or utility of a unit within a rapidly expanding dimension of a larger recursive system.

A scientific paper may become less valuable when millions of additional papers are produced.

A page of mathematical proof may become less valuable when proof generation becomes abundant while interpretation remains scarce.

A line of software code may become less valuable when software generation outpaces maintenance and comprehension.

A million dollars may become less economically meaningful when valuation growth significantly outpaces practical integration and deployment.

In each case, the underlying artifact may remain entirely real and often highly valuable.

The imbalance emerges because one component of a larger system is scaling substantially faster than the others.

To describe this process, the paper adopts a triadic framework consisting of Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

Knowledge represents cognitive production, discovery, information, automation, and scientific output.

Resources represent capital, investment, compute availability, and other forms of accumulated economic capability.

Infrastructure represents the physical, institutional, organizational, and human structures required to absorb, interpret, regulate, maintain, and utilize knowledge and resources effectively.

Under balanced conditions these dimensions reinforce one another through a recursive cycle.

Under sufficiently asymmetric growth rates, however, structural imbalances emerge.

The resulting inflationary effects may appear in scientific research, software development, cybersecurity, financial markets, educational systems, and AI ecosystems.

Unlike classical bubble narratives, triadic inflation does not require underlying technologies to be fraudulent, ineffective, or fundamentally overvalued.

The technology may be real.

The progress may be real.

The knowledge may be real.

The capital may be real.

The imbalance emerges because recursive growth accelerates faster than the structures capable of integrating that growth.

Consequently, the collapse of a financial valuation bubble does not necessarily eliminate the underlying imbalance. The generated knowledge, software, infrastructure commitments, and societal adaptations continue to exist. In this sense, triadic inflation describes a structural rather than purely financial phenomenon.

The sections that follow formalize the concept of triadic inflation, establish its relationship to closure overshoot and temporal misalignment, and examine several contemporary examples drawn from artificial intelligence, scientific research, cybersecurity, and economic systems.

Related Work and Conceptual Context

The concept of Triadic Inflation does not emerge in isolation.

Many of its individual components have appeared previously in economics, innovation theory, complexity science, systems theory, and the broader Triadic Cosmos ecosystem.

The contribution of the present paper is therefore not the discovery of entirely new phenomena, but rather the proposal of a unified triadic interpretation connecting several well-known observations through a single recursive framework.

Economic thought has long recognized that technological progress can create structural imbalances.

Perhaps one of the earliest examples appears in the work of John Maynard Keynes, who discussed the possibility of technological unemployment arising from productivity improvements that outpace the capacity of economic systems to absorb them.

Although formulated in a different language, such arguments already hint at the possibility that different components of a larger system may evolve at different rates.

Similarly, innovation theorists such as Joseph Schumpeter described technological progress as a process of creative destruction in which new productive structures systematically displace older ones. Again, the central observation involves imbalance and adaptation rather than static equilibrium.

More recent theories emphasize the role of knowledge itself as a fundamental driver of growth.

The endogenous growth literature, particularly the work of Paul Romer, treats knowledge as a non-rival resource whose accumulation drives long-term economic expansion.

Likewise, the work of Michael Kremer links technological progress to the number of potential inventors and producers of ideas.

Such theories help explain why increases in cognitive capacity can accelerate innovation and growth.

However, they generally focus on the generation of knowledge rather than on the structural consequences that follow when knowledge production itself becomes a bottleneck-shifting process.

Complex systems research has also identified related mechanisms.

Feedback loops, carrying capacities, overshoot phenomena, emergence, and non-linear system responses are widely studied across ecology, cybernetics, economics, and network science.

Many such frameworks recognize that rapidly growing subsystems can place pressure on larger coordinating structures.

The present paper adopts a similar perspective but focuses specifically on recursive triadic closures and the observable inflationary patterns that emerge when those closures become imbalanced.

Classical bubble theory provides another closely related perspective.

Traditional analyses generally focus on situations in which valuations become detached from underlying fundamentals. Asset prices rise faster than the economic realities they are intended to represent, eventually producing corrections or crashes.

This paper retains that insight but proposes a broader interpretation.

Valuation inflation is viewed as one possible manifestation of a more general phenomenon.

Knowledge, resources, and infrastructure can each exhibit inflationary behavior whenever recursive growth causes one dimension to outpace the adaptive capacity of the others.

Within the Triadic Cosmos ecosystem, these ideas connect directly to two previous theoretical developments.

The Closure Overshoot Law proposes that recursive closures may temporarily exceed their carrying capacity, generating instability and correction dynamics.

The Temporal Misalignment framework proposes that different components of a recursive system often evolve on incompatible timescales, creating persistent structural tension even when all components are individually functional.

Triadic Inflation extends these observations by focusing on their observable consequences.

Rather than asking why overshoot occurs or how temporal mismatch emerges, the present paper examines how such imbalances become visible through changes in the scarcity, value, and utility of units generated within the system.

The framework developed in the sections that follow therefore should not be interpreted as a replacement for existing theories of technological change, innovation, bubbles, or systemic instability.

Instead, it proposes a common recursive structure through which many of these phenomena may be interpreted.

This structure will be formalized through a triadic cycle linking Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure, and subsequently used to analyze a series of contemporary examples drawn from artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, scientific research, and technological infrastructure.

Foundational Principles

The preceding discussion positioned Triadic Inflation within the broader literature on growth, innovation, bubbles, complexity, and recursive systems.

Before introducing the specific Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure cycle, it is useful to establish the central principles upon which the present framework is built.

Three concepts form the foundation of the theory developed throughout this paper:

  • Triadic Inflation

  • The Scarcity Conservation Principle

  • The Bottleneck Migration Principle

Together these principles describe how recursive systems generate, relocate, and reveal scarcity during periods of rapid growth.

Triadic Inflation

In economics, inflation is most commonly associated with a decline in the purchasing power of money caused by an increase in monetary supply.

More generally, however, the term inflation has historically been used to describe situations in which rapid expansion reduces the relative scarcity of individual units within an expanding domain.

Examples include monetary inflation, grade inflation, credential inflation, information inflation, and even cosmological inflation, where a system undergoes a period of extremely rapid expansion.

The present paper adopts this broader interpretation.

Triadic Inflation is defined as:

Triadic Inflation occurs when one dimension of a recursive system expands substantially faster than the adaptive capacity of the remaining dimensions, reducing the relative scarcity, significance, or utility of newly produced units within the expanding dimension.

The concept therefore extends beyond monetary value.

Knowledge may experience inflation when discoveries become easier to produce than to understand.

Resources may experience inflation when capital accumulates faster than it can be deployed.

Infrastructure may experience inflation when structural capacity grows faster than future utilization requirements.

In all cases, the defining characteristic is not monetary expansion but the emergence of abundance relative to integration capacity.

The Scarcity Conservation Principle

A central observation motivating the present framework is that successful growth rarely eliminates scarcity entirely.

Instead, scarcity tends to migrate between components of the system.

This observation is formalized as the Scarcity Conservation Principle.

Scarcity within a recursive adaptive system is not typically eliminated by growth. Instead, growth reduces scarcity locally while creating new scarcity within slower-adapting dimensions of the same system.

When scientific discovery accelerates, interpretation becomes scarce.

When capital becomes abundant, deployment becomes scarce.

When infrastructure expands, utilization becomes scarce.

The specific bottleneck changes, but scarcity itself persists.

The principle does not imply that scarcity is literally conserved as a physical quantity.

Rather, it states that recursive systems continually relocate their dominant constraints.

Progress therefore transforms scarcity more often than it eliminates it.

The Bottleneck Migration Principle

The Scarcity Conservation Principle implies a related operational consequence.

As the production capacity of one component increases, system performance becomes increasingly constrained by neighboring components whose adaptation rate remains lower.

This process is referred to as Bottleneck Migration.

Bottlenecks tend to migrate toward the slowest adapting dimension of a recursive system.

Historically, many scientific, technological, and economic systems have been constrained by knowledge generation itself.

As knowledge production accelerates, interpretation, verification, governance, and integration increasingly become the limiting factors.

Similarly, when capital allocation accelerates, deployment and coordination become bottlenecks.

When infrastructure expands rapidly, future utilization may become the primary constraint.

Triadic Inflation may therefore be interpreted as the observable signal produced by bottleneck migration within recursive systems.

Relationship to the Ecosystem Framework

Within the broader Triadic Cosmos ecosystem, the three papers of the present trilogy describe successive levels of abstraction.

The Closure Overshoot Law proposes that recursive closures can exceed their carrying capacity.

Temporal Misalignment explains how such overshoot emerges through different adaptation rates among coupled dimensions.

The present paper introduces Triadic Inflation as the observable manifestation of these processes.

The resulting relationship may be summarized as:

Closure → Overshoot → Temporal Misalignment → Bottleneck Migration → Triadic Inflation

where bottleneck migration describes the relocation of scarcity and triadic inflation describes the observable reduction in scarcity within the rapidly expanding dimension.

The Knowledge, Resource and Infrastructure Triadic Cycle

The Triadic Cosmos ecosystem frequently models complex adaptive systems through interacting triadic structures connected by recursive feedback mechanisms.

Within economic, scientific, technological, and societal contexts, one of the most fundamental of these structures consists of three dimensions:

  • Knowledge

  • Resources

  • Infrastructure

Knowledge represents information, understanding, scientific discoveries, technical expertise, organizational know-how, automation capabilities, software systems, and all other forms of cognitive output.

Resources represent accumulated economic capability, including capital, investment, compute capacity, labor allocation, energy expenditure, research funding, and other means through which knowledge can be converted into action.

Infrastructure represents the structures that allow knowledge and resources to be deployed effectively. These structures may be physical, institutional, organizational, educational, regulatory, computational, or human in nature.

Importantly, the three dimensions are not independent. They form a recursive cycle.

Knowledge enables the creation of new resources. Scientific discoveries, engineering advances, organizational improvements, and technological innovation all increase the ability of a system to generate economic and productive output.

Resources subsequently enable the expansion of infrastructure. Capital, investment, energy, labor, and material resources can be transformed into factories, datacenters, educational institutions, transportation networks, communication systems, regulatory frameworks, and other structures that increase the capacity of the system.

Infrastructure in turn enables the generation of new knowledge. Schools produce skilled workers. Universities produce researchers. Datacenters enable computation. Communication networks facilitate collaboration. Regulatory and organizational systems allow increasingly complex forms of coordination.

The cycle can therefore be represented schematically as:

Knowledge → Resources → Infrastructure → Knowledge

The relationship is not linear but recursive.

Each successful iteration of the cycle increases the potential scale of future iterations. New knowledge can generate additional resources, additional resources can expand infrastructure, and improved infrastructure can accelerate future knowledge generation.

Historically, many of the most significant periods of human development can be interpreted through this lens. Scientific discoveries created new economic opportunities. Economic growth enabled infrastructure expansion. Expanded infrastructure supported larger populations, more education, more research, and ultimately further scientific progress.

In balanced systems, this recursive cycle can remain remarkably stable for extended periods of time. Knowledge, resources, and infrastructure continue reinforcing one another and collectively expand the carrying capacity of the larger system.

The very mechanism that enables growth, however, also creates the possibility of instability.

If one dimension begins scaling significantly faster than the others, the recursive cycle becomes increasingly asymmetric. Under such conditions the system may enter regimes characterized by structural imbalance, temporal misalignment, or overshoot.

The remainder of this paper focuses on one particular manifestation of such imbalance: triadic inflation.

The Principle of Triadic Inflation

Classical inflation is most commonly associated with money. When the supply of money grows significantly faster than the supply of goods and services, the purchasing power of individual monetary units declines.

The concept proposed in this paper generalizes this intuition beyond currency.

Within recursive triadic systems, inflation can emerge whenever the production rate of one dimension substantially exceeds the adaptive capacity of the remaining dimensions.

In such situations, the affected dimension becomes increasingly abundant while the structures required to utilize, process, integrate, or absorb that output remain comparatively constrained.

The result is a reduction in the relative value, utility, scarcity, or significance of individual units produced by the expanding dimension.

This phenomenon is referred to as Triadic Inflation.

Consider a balanced Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure cycle:

Knowledge → Resources → Infrastructure → Knowledge

Under balanced growth conditions, all three dimensions reinforce one another while remaining broadly synchronized.

New knowledge creates resources.

New resources expand infrastructure.

New infrastructure enables the production of additional knowledge.

Growth remains sustainable because the carrying capacity of the overall system expands alongside the outputs produced by the individual components.

Triadic inflation appears when this balance is disrupted.

If the growth rate of one dimension becomes substantially larger than the adaptation rate of the other two dimensions, recursive amplification begins producing an oversupply of that dimension relative to the closure as a whole.

The resulting imbalance may persist even when the inflated dimension contains genuine value.

Indeed, triadic inflation often emerges precisely because something important, useful, or transformative has been discovered.

Knowledge Inflation

Knowledge inflation occurs when cognitive production grows faster than the ability of a system to absorb it.

Examples include:

  • scientific publications that cannot realistically be reviewed or integrated;

  • software generated faster than it can be maintained;

  • mathematical proofs whose formal correctness outpaces human understanding;

  • vulnerability discovery that exceeds remediation capacity.

In such cases, knowledge continues to be valuable.

However, the relative value of an additional unit of knowledge declines because the system’s capacity for interpretation and utilization becomes the limiting factor.

Resource Inflation

Resource inflation occurs when capital, investment, valuation, or productive capability grows faster than the available infrastructure able to deploy it effectively.

Examples include:

  • large-scale speculative funding waves;

  • infrastructure commitments based on anticipated future demand;

  • valuation growth that substantially exceeds current integration capacity;

  • ecosystems that prioritize scale acquisition before achieving sustainable monetization.

Importantly, resource inflation does not imply that the underlying technology lacks value.

On the contrary, the technology may be genuinely transformative.

The imbalance arises because expectations concerning future resource creation outpace the structural mechanisms required to capture that value.

Infrastructure Inflation

Infrastructure inflation is less common but can emerge when the remaining dimensions fail to keep pace with structural requirements.

As knowledge and resources accelerate, infrastructure itself may become the primary scarce asset.

Examples include:

  • datacenter capacity;

  • electrical grids;

  • educational systems;

  • regulatory institutions;

  • expert review communities;

  • human cognitive attention.

In such cases, infrastructure becomes increasingly valuable precisely because it grows more slowly than the dimensions depending upon it.

A defining characteristic of triadic inflation is that the phenomenon does not require deception, irrationality, fraud, or technological failure.

The technology may function exactly as intended.

The knowledge may be correct.

The resources may be real.

Inflation emerges because recursive growth within one dimension exceeds the integration capacity of the larger triadic closure.

Consequently, triadic inflation should not be understood primarily as a financial phenomenon.

It is a structural phenomenon.

The apparent inflation of knowledge, resources, or infrastructure is therefore best interpreted not as the root cause of the problem but as an observable signal that the recursive cycle has entered a state of increasing imbalance.

Inflation versus Classical Bubble Dynamics

The concept of a bubble occupies a central position in economic thinking.

Classical bubble theories typically describe situations in which the perceived value of an asset diverges significantly from its fundamental value. During such periods, prices rise more rapidly than the underlying economic reality appears to justify. Eventually a correction occurs, valuations decline, and the system moves toward a new equilibrium.

In its simplest form, the mechanism is largely two-dimensional.

Perceived Value ≫ Fundamental Value

The resulting instability is therefore primarily interpreted as a valuation problem.

Once valuations adjust, the central imbalance is often considered to have been resolved.

Triadic inflation differs in an important way.

The primary concern is not whether an asset, company, technology, or scientific result possesses genuine value. In fact, triadic inflation may emerge precisely because a discovery, technology, or capability proves to be genuinely useful.

The source of instability is not valuation alone.

The source of instability is structural imbalance within a recursive Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure closure.

Knowledge → Resources → Infrastructure → Knowledge

Asymmetric growth may therefore occur even when all three components are real and valuable.

Knowledge may grow faster than infrastructure.

Resources may grow faster than infrastructure.

Infrastructure may grow faster than future utilization.

The resulting imbalance produces inflationary effects throughout the system.

Consider the case of scientific research.

A classical bubble interpretation would ask whether a research result is correct or incorrect.

A triadic inflation interpretation asks a different question:

Can new knowledge be generated faster than the scientific community can verify, understand, teach, integrate, and apply it?

The problem is no longer valuation.

The problem is carrying capacity.

Similarly, in software development the relevant question is not whether generated code works.

Much of it may work perfectly well.

The relevant question becomes whether software can be reviewed, maintained, secured, documented, and integrated at the same rate as it is being produced.

Again, the bottleneck emerges within the larger closure rather than within the generated artifact itself.

This distinction is particularly important because a collapse in valuation does not necessarily eliminate the underlying imbalance.

Suppose a technology bubble collapses.

Capital may disappear.

Valuations may decline.

Investment may slow.

Yet the generated knowledge frequently remains.

The software remains.

The infrastructures already constructed remain.

The institutional pressures created by the technology may remain.

Consequently, the recursive system does not automatically return to its previous state.

Only one component of the closure has contracted.

The remaining components continue to shape future dynamics.

In this sense, a classical bubble can be interpreted as a special case of triadic inflation.

Valuation inflation represents one particular form of imbalance within a larger recursive closure.

Triadic inflation extends the concept by recognizing that inflationary effects may appear simultaneously within cognitive systems, scientific communities, technological ecosystems, labor markets, infrastructure networks, and financial systems.

The focus therefore shifts from valuation alone toward the broader question of structural balance.

Rather than asking whether expectations exceed reality, triadic inflation asks whether the different dimensions of a recursive system are evolving at compatible rates.

When they are not, inflation, temporal misalignment, and overshoot emerge as different manifestations of the same underlying process.

Link to Overshoot and Temporal Misalignment

The concept of Triadic Inflation did not emerge independently.

It can be understood as a direct consequence of two previously proposed frameworks: the Closure Overshoot Law and Temporal Misalignment.

The Closure Overshoot Law argues that recursive closures may temporarily grow beyond the carrying capacity of the larger systems that contain them. Recursive growth amplifies itself through positive feedback mechanisms. Under sufficiently favorable conditions, the closure begins expanding faster than the structures responsible for stabilizing it.

Temporal Misalignment introduces a related but distinct observation.

Different components within a recursive system rarely evolve at the same rate.

Knowledge may expand on timescales of days or months.

Capital allocation may respond on timescales of months or quarters.

Infrastructure often requires years to develop.

Educational systems may require decades.

Cultural and institutional adaptation may require generations.

Consequently, even if all dimensions of a system are ultimately capable of reaching a stable equilibrium, they may remain significantly out of phase for long periods of time.

Triadic Inflation emerges naturally from the combination of these two effects.

Consider a recursive Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure closure.

If all three dimensions expand at approximately comparable rates, the system remains broadly balanced.

However, if one dimension begins accelerating through recursive feedback, while the remaining dimensions continue adapting on slower timescales, the closure enters a state of temporal misalignment.

The resulting imbalance produces overshoot.

The observable manifestation of that overshoot is what this paper refers to as inflation.

Temporal Misalignment → Overshoot → Triadic Inflation

In this interpretation, inflation is not the underlying cause.

Inflation is the visible symptom.

The deeper mechanism remains the mismatch between growth rates within the recursive closure.

For this reason, triadic inflation can be viewed as the empirical and observable counterpart of the more general Overshoot and Temporal Misalignment frameworks.

The relationship between the three concepts can be summarized as follows:

  • Closure Overshoot Law describes why recursive systems can exceed their carrying capacity.

  • Temporal Misalignment describes how different components of the closure evolve on incompatible timescales.

  • Triadic Inflation describes how these imbalances become visible through the changing value, scarcity, and significance of Knowledge, Resource, or Infrastructure units.

Viewed in this way, inflation is not restricted to monetary systems.

Any recursive triadic closure may exhibit inflationary phenomena.

Knowledge can experience inflation when cognitive production exceeds integration capacity.

Resources can experience inflation when capital expansion exceeds productive deployment capacity.

Infrastructure can experience inflation when investment in structural capacity exceeds future utilization requirements.

The resulting dynamics are particularly visible within contemporary AI ecosystems.

Recent examples include rapid valuation expansion within frontier AI companies, large-scale datacenter construction, accelerated software production, increased vulnerability discovery, and the emergence of large formally verified mathematical proofs.

These phenomena appear superficially unrelated.

Within the triadic framework, however, they can be interpreted as different manifestations of the same underlying process: recursive growth generating structural imbalance between Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

Triadic Inflation therefore completes a progression of increasingly concrete descriptions of recursive imbalance:

Closure → Overshoot → Temporal Misalignment → Triadic Inflation

The Closure Overshoot Law describes the existence of the phenomenon.

Temporal Misalignment describes the mechanism through which it emerges.

Triadic Inflation describes how the phenomenon becomes observable in real-world systems.

Knowledge Inflation Examples

Knowledge Inflation occurs when the production of new knowledge grows faster than the capacity of individuals, organizations, and institutions to interpret, validate, integrate, teach, and utilize that knowledge.

Historically, the generation of knowledge was constrained by human cognitive capacity. Scientific discoveries, mathematical proofs, engineering advances, and technological innovations emerged at rates that remained broadly compatible with the ability of societies to absorb and build upon them.

Artificial intelligence introduces a potentially significant change to this relationship.

The scarcity of knowledge generation itself is beginning to decline. Software can be generated automatically. Scientific hypotheses can be proposed automatically. Mathematical proofs can increasingly be explored, formalized, and verified with machine assistance. Vulnerabilities can be identified at scales previously impractical for human analysts.

As a result, the primary bottleneck begins to shift.

Throughout much of human history, discovery represented the dominant constraint. New knowledge was scarce, while the institutional structures required to process it generally possessed sufficient capacity to absorb the resulting output.

Increasingly, however, the challenge may no longer be the creation of knowledge itself.

The challenge becomes interpretation.

Attention becomes scarce.

Verification becomes scarce.

Integration becomes scarce.

Education becomes scarce.

In triadic terms, the production of cognitive artifacts begins to accelerate faster than the supporting structures responsible for incorporating those artifacts into the broader ecosystem:

Knowledge ≫ Infrastructure

Importantly, infrastructure in this context extends beyond physical systems. It includes the human, institutional, educational, organizational, regulatory, and cultural mechanisms that transform individual discoveries into shared and usable knowledge.

Knowledge Inflation therefore does not imply that newly generated knowledge is incorrect, low quality, or without value.

Quite the opposite.

The phenomenon frequently emerges precisely because knowledge generation becomes increasingly successful.

The resulting inflationary effect arises when the rate of production begins exceeding the rate of absorption.

The scarcity of knowledge declines.

The scarcity of understanding remains.

The following examples illustrate different manifestations of this process across mathematics, scientific research, and cybersecurity.

The Riemann Hypothesis

The events surrounding the Riemann Hypothesis in late 2026 provide a particularly striking example of Knowledge Inflation within advanced mathematics.

In November 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, publicly claimed a proof of both the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis developed in collaboration with Claude. Rather than appearing as a conventional mathematical manuscript, the result was presented primarily as a massive Lean formalization composed of multiple archives and millions of lines of machine-verifiable proof code. The accompanying human-readable representation was described as exceeding 250,000 pages.

At nearly the same time, an independent research effort emerged.

Mathematician Alessandro Masullo had spent approximately twelve years developing a separate proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. His manuscript contained roughly 940 pages and was originally intended to undergo a longer process of refinement and verification before public release. Following the Anthropic announcement, the manuscript was published earlier than planned in order to enter the broader scientific discussion.

The specific correctness of any individual proof claim is largely irrelevant to the argument.

Viewed superficially, the episode appears to be a comparison between human and machine-assisted mathematics.

From the perspective of Triadic Inflation, however, the more important observation concerns the changing economics of knowledge production.

Historically, deep mathematical results were constrained by both the availability of researchers capable of producing them and the capacity of the mathematical community to verify and assimilate them. This naturally created extended periods during which discoveries could be reviewed, debated, refined, taught, and gradually integrated into the wider body of mathematical knowledge.

The events of late 2026 suggest a different dynamic.

Large formally verified proofs can now potentially be generated, formalized, and distributed on timescales that differ substantially from traditional publication and review cycles. Knowledge production begins to accelerate relative to interpretation, education, verification, and institutional integration.

In triadic terms:

Knowledge ≫ Infrastructure

where infrastructure refers not to hardware or datacenters, but to the human and institutional structures responsible for understanding, teaching, validating, and integrating mathematical discoveries.

An important additional consideration concerns representation.

The enormous size of the Lean proof should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence that the underlying mathematical argument itself is hundreds of times larger than conventional proofs. Formal proof assistants often require explicit specification of intermediate reasoning steps that human mathematicians routinely omit. Consequently, a comparison between a 940-page manuscript and a 250,000-page machine representation is best understood as a comparison between different forms of knowledge encoding rather than a direct comparison of conceptual complexity.

Nevertheless, the episode reveals a genuine inflationary shift.

The scarcity of proof generation declines.

The scarcity of formal verification declines.

The scarcity of publication declines.

Human interpretation does not decline at the same rate.

Attention, comprehension, validation, communication, and integration remain comparatively scarce resources.

The significance of the episode therefore extends far beyond the Riemann Hypothesis itself.

For centuries, one of the primary bottlenecks of advanced mathematics was the generation of profound mathematical insight. Recent developments suggest that the bottleneck may increasingly migrate toward the interpretation and integration of those insights.

Whether any individual proof program ultimately proves correct or incorrect is therefore only part of the story.

The more fundamental observation is that mathematical knowledge may be entering a regime in which proof production scales substantially faster than the infrastructures responsible for absorbing it.

The scarcity of discovery decreases.

The scarcity of understanding remains.

Large-Scale Mathematical Generation

While the Riemann Hypothesis episode illustrates Knowledge Inflation through a single highly visible case, recent developments in AI-assisted mathematics suggest that the phenomenon may be considerably broader.

In 2026, OpenAI publicly released hundreds of mathematical research papers spanning multiple areas of mathematics. The resulting collection included novel conjectures, proof strategies, computational explorations, heuristic arguments, and theoretical investigations across a wide range of disciplines.

The significance of this development does not depend upon the ultimate correctness or importance of every individual paper.

The more important observation concerns scale.

Historically, mathematical research output was constrained by the number of trained mathematicians capable of producing original results. Mathematical discovery remained fundamentally tied to human cognitive throughput.

AI systems potentially alter this relationship.

A growing fraction of mathematical exploration can now be delegated to computational systems capable of generating large collections of hypotheses, derivations, proof sketches, formalizations, and research directions in parallel.

The intellectual bottleneck therefore begins to shift.

The central challenge is increasingly no longer the generation of mathematical artifacts themselves.

It becomes the interpretation, verification, and integration of those artifacts into the broader body of mathematical knowledge.

In triadic terms:

Knowledge ≫ Infrastructure

where infrastructure consists of the mathematical community itself: reviewers, educators, journals, conferences, students, experts, and the broader mechanisms through which mathematical discoveries are validated and transmitted.

Several widely discussed results from these releases illustrate the phenomenon.

One notable example involved new research related to the Riemann Hypothesis, including improvements that narrowed regions in which nontrivial zeros may occur. Such results generated significant interest and discussion throughout the mathematical community.

Yet shortly afterwards, substantially more ambitious proof claims concerning both the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis emerged. Attention rapidly shifted toward these newer claims.

Whether any of these individual results ultimately prove correct is not the central issue.

The more interesting observation is the compression of scientific attention.

Work that might previously have dominated discussion for years can now find itself competing with successive waves of new mathematical output arriving on increasingly shorter timescales.

Questions therefore emerge that would have seemed unusual in earlier scientific eras.

Can important mathematical discoveries become difficult to fully absorb before the next generation of discoveries appears?

Can attention become scarcer than theorem generation?

Can interpretation become scarcer than proof production?

Can educational and institutional processes adapt to exponentially increasing cognitive output?

These questions illustrate the essence of Knowledge Inflation.

The problem is not that the generated mathematics lacks value.

Indeed, the mathematics may be highly valuable.

The challenge is that the rate of knowledge generation may increasingly exceed the capacity of surrounding institutions to review, replicate, verify, teach, communicate, and integrate that knowledge.

The scarcity of discovery declines.

The scarcity of attention remains.

The bottleneck moves.

Cybersecurity

While mathematics provides examples of Knowledge Inflation within scientific research, cybersecurity demonstrates that similar dynamics are already visible within large-scale industrial ecosystems.

Indeed, cybersecurity may represent one of the earliest domains in which the consequences of accelerating knowledge production can be observed directly and operationally.

The reason is relatively simple.

Vulnerability discovery, validation, remediation, deployment, and monitoring form a tightly coupled feedback loop whose outputs are often measurable on short timescales.

Historically, the dominant challenge in cybersecurity was the discovery of vulnerabilities themselves.

Unknown exploits could remain hidden for years. Attack surfaces were only partially understood, auditing was expensive, and identifying critical weaknesses often required substantial expertise and manual effort.

As a result, the rate of vulnerability discovery remained broadly compatible with the capacity of organizations to evaluate and address the problems that were found.

Recent developments have altered this relationship.

Automated scanning, increasingly sophisticated security tooling, large-scale code analysis, and AI-assisted vulnerability research have dramatically increased the rate at which potential weaknesses can be identified. Security researchers, corporations, governments, and even attackers now possess tools capable of exploring software ecosystems at scales previously impractical for human analysts alone.

The result is a familiar triadic pattern:

Knowledge ≫ Infrastructure

Here, knowledge consists of discovered vulnerabilities, attack strategies, behavioral observations, threat intelligence, and security insights.

Infrastructure consists of the organizations, engineering teams, deployment procedures, testing frameworks, governance systems, and operational processes required to act upon that knowledge.

The scarcity of discovering vulnerabilities decreases.

The scarcity of remediation does not.

Consequently, the bottleneck migrates from discovery toward response.

Every newly discovered vulnerability initiates a secondary sequence of activities constrained by existing infrastructure.

A patch must be designed.

The patch must be implemented.

Testing must be performed.

Deployment procedures must be planned.

Dependent systems must be validated.

Potential downtime must be managed.

Organizations and users must adapt.

Unlike knowledge generation, many of these processes cannot be accelerated arbitrarily without introducing additional risk.

As a result, vulnerability remediation frequently scales more slowly than vulnerability discovery.

The imbalance may become self-reinforcing.

New fixes occasionally introduce new vulnerabilities.

Security updates can generate unexpected system interactions.

Additional monitoring and verification become necessary.

Increasing security knowledge therefore does not automatically reduce system complexity.

Under some circumstances, it may increase it.

An analogous pattern appears in the development of advanced AI systems themselves.

Safety researchers have repeatedly reported unexpected strategies, novel behaviors, specification gaming, and other unforeseen interactions that emerge during testing and deployment.

Whether such discoveries ultimately prove benign or serious is less important than the structural pattern they reveal.

The space of known behaviors expands faster than the surrounding safety, governance, monitoring, and evaluation infrastructures designed to understand and manage them.

Once again, production exceeds integration.

Knowledge grows faster than closure capacity.

A particularly important consequence follows.

As vulnerability discovery accelerates, organizations may become more cautious rather than less.

Security backlogs increase.

Regression testing requirements expand.

Compliance obligations grow.

Risk management becomes more complex.

Deployment cycles may slow rather than accelerate.

Each additional discovery creates further work for the surrounding infrastructure.

Paradoxically, greater knowledge generation may therefore reduce the effective rate at which changes can safely be deployed.

Knowledge growth eventually encounters a structural bottleneck.

Within the framework of Triadic Inflation, cybersecurity therefore provides a particularly concrete demonstration of Knowledge Inflation.

The additional knowledge is not incorrect.

The discoveries are not without value.

On the contrary, vulnerability discovery is generally beneficial.

The inflation emerges because security-relevant knowledge can now be generated faster than institutions, organizations, and technical systems can absorb and act upon it.

Cybersecurity therefore illustrates a broader phenomenon that increasingly appears throughout advanced technological ecosystems:

Knowledge ↑  ↑ ↑

Integration↑

The bottleneck does not disappear.

It migrates.

From discovery toward response.

From generation toward integration.

From knowledge toward closure.

Common Pattern

The three examples presented above differ substantially in domain, timescale, and scale of operation.

The Riemann Hypothesis episode concerns a small number of exceptionally deep mathematical results.

Large-scale mathematical generation concerns the industrialization of research itself through AI-assisted discovery.

Cybersecurity concerns operational knowledge production occurring continuously within technological ecosystems.

Yet despite these differences, all three examples exhibit the same underlying structural pattern.

Historically, the primary bottleneck was knowledge generation.

Mathematical insight was scarce.

Research output was scarce.

Vulnerability discovery was scarce.

Most institutional structures evolved under the assumption that the production of new knowledge would remain the dominant constraint.

Artificial intelligence begins altering this assumption.

Knowledge generation becomes increasingly scalable.

Proofs can be formalized more rapidly.

Research directions can be explored in parallel.

Software can be analyzed automatically.

Vulnerabilities can be discovered at unprecedented rates.

As a result, the scarcity structure of the system changes.

The challenge is no longer necessarily producing additional knowledge.

The challenge becomes absorbing it.

Attention becomes scarce.

Verification becomes scarce.

Interpretation becomes scarce.

Integration becomes scarce.

Education becomes scarce.

The bottleneck migrates from discovery toward incorporation.

In triadic terms, the same imbalance emerges repeatedly:

Knowledge ≫ Infrastructure

where infrastructure consists of the human and institutional mechanisms required to transform information into shared and actionable knowledge.

Importantly, this does not imply that the generated knowledge lacks value.

Indeed, the opposite is often true.

The more useful a knowledge-generating system becomes, the more rapidly the supporting infrastructure may struggle to keep pace.

Knowledge Inflation therefore should not be interpreted as a failure of knowledge production.

It should be interpreted as a consequence of its success.

The resulting inflationary signal emerges because production begins to scale faster than absorption.

The scarcity of discovery declines.

The scarcity of understanding remains.

The bottleneck moves.

Resource Inflation Examples

Resource Inflation occurs when financial resources, investment capacity, or economic commitments grow substantially faster than the infrastructure required to deploy them effectively.

Historically, resources were largely constrained by existing productive capacity. Investment generally followed demonstrated opportunities, observable demand, and proven capabilities. While speculation has always existed, large-scale resource allocation remained closely tied to present economic activity.

Technological revolutions can alter this relationship.

When transformative technologies emerge, investors increasingly allocate resources not according to present capabilities, but according to anticipated future capabilities. Expected future productivity becomes capitalized into present economic resources.

In effect, future possibilities are converted into current investment.

From the perspective of the triadic cycle, this creates a distinctive form of recursive amplification:

Future Knowledge → Present Resources → Expanded Infrastructure → Additional Knowledge

The resulting process can become highly self-reinforcing.

Additional resources fund new infrastructure.

Infrastructure accelerates knowledge creation.

New knowledge increases future expectations.

Those expectations attract further resources.

Importantly, Resource Inflation does not imply irrationality.

The anticipated capabilities may eventually materialize.

The underlying technology may prove genuinely transformative.

The inflationary phenomenon emerges because resources can often scale faster than the institutions, markets, regulatory systems, and social structures required to absorb them productively.

In triadic terms:

Resources ≫ Infrastructure

The following examples illustrate different manifestations of this process across historical, industrial, and contemporary technological contexts.

Railway Expansion and Industrial Resource Inflation

One of the earliest large-scale examples of Resource Inflation occurred during the railway expansions of the nineteenth century.

Railroads represented a genuinely transformative technology. They dramatically reduced transportation costs, enabled larger markets, accelerated industrialization, and reshaped economic geography.

Investors recognized these possibilities and began committing enormous financial resources to railway construction.

Importantly, much of this investment was based upon anticipated future growth rather than existing demand.

Future transportation capacity was capitalized into present financial resources.

As railway development accelerated, capital flowed into land acquisition, construction projects, steel production, stations, rolling stock, and associated infrastructure.

Many railway systems ultimately proved enormously valuable.

Yet periods of excessive capital allocation also emerged, producing overbuilding, duplication of routes, speculative financing, and significant financial instability.

Viewed through the lens of Triadic Inflation, the central issue was not whether railways possessed value.

They clearly did.

The issue was whether resources were expanding faster than the broader economic infrastructure could absorb and utilize them.

The resulting dynamics provide an early example of Resource Inflation within a rapidly developing technological ecosystem.

Future Capability Capitalization

Modern technology companies provide a contemporary illustration of the same mechanism.

Companies such as Tesla and SpaceX are frequently valued not only on the basis of their current revenues, products, or operational performance, but also on expectations regarding future capabilities.

Investors often price anticipated outcomes including large-scale automation, autonomous transportation, robotics, reusable launch economics, satellite infrastructure, planetary exploration, and associated technological spillovers.

In such cases, valuation becomes a mechanism through which expectations about future knowledge and future productive capacity are transformed into present resources.

Those resources can subsequently be deployed toward hiring, research, infrastructure development, manufacturing capacity, and technological experimentation.

The phenomenon does not necessarily imply that such valuations are incorrect.

Many of the anticipated capabilities may ultimately materialize.

The relevant observation is structural rather than predictive.

Resources can be mobilized and deployed long before the surrounding economic ecosystem has fully adapted to the future technologies being financed.

As a result, the growth rate of available resources may exceed the rate at which infrastructure, institutions, and markets can integrate the resulting transformation.

Resource Inflation therefore concerns the timing of resource allocation rather than the ultimate success or failure of the underlying technology.

Frontier AI and Accelerating Resource Inflation

The dynamics become particularly visible within contemporary AI ecosystems.

A prominent example is provided by frontier AI companies such as Anthropic, whose valuation reflects not only current products and revenues but also expectations regarding future artificial intelligence capabilities.

Investors are not merely pricing present systems.

They are pricing anticipated future outcomes including increasingly capable AI agents, large-scale automation, accelerated scientific research, software generation, and the possibility of more advanced machine intelligence.

In this sense, future knowledge creation becomes directly translated into present economic resources.

Unlike many earlier technological revolutions, however, AI introduces a unique feedback mechanism.

The technology itself may contribute to the production of additional knowledge.

Consequently, both Knowledge and Resources can begin accelerating simultaneously.

The bottleneck increasingly shifts toward Infrastructure.

Infrastructure in this context extends far beyond datacenters and compute clusters.

It includes educational systems, labor markets, regulatory adaptation, organizational transformation, scientific validation, consumer adoption, governance structures, and broader forms of social coordination.

Even if future AI capabilities ultimately prove genuine, these structures cannot necessarily adapt at the same rate.

This distinction is important.

Triadic Inflation does not imply that frontier AI firms are necessarily overvalued.

Nor does it imply that future AI capabilities are unattainable.

The technology may ultimately exceed current expectations.

Resource Inflation can nevertheless emerge because resource allocation responds to anticipated future capabilities faster than surrounding infrastructures can absorb the resulting transformation.

An additional characteristic of AI ecosystems further reinforces this pattern.

Many AI services are currently offered at prices that appear low relative to the infrastructure required to provide them. Adoption, ecosystem growth, developer engagement, and platform expansion often appear to be prioritized over immediate profitability.

Future value is therefore increasingly capitalized into present resource allocation while short-term monetization remains comparatively limited.

Viewed through the framework of Triadic Inflation, contemporary AI may therefore represent one of the clearest examples of Resource Inflation currently visible within the global economy.

Common Pattern

The three examples reveal a common pattern.

Railways, advanced industrial firms, and frontier AI ecosystems differ substantially in technology, timescale, and historical context.

Yet all exhibit the same structural dynamic.

Future capability becomes present capital.

Resources expand before the surrounding infrastructure can fully absorb the resulting transformation.

The resulting imbalance does not imply that the technology lacks value.

Indeed, many of the underlying innovations may ultimately prove highly successful.

The inflationary signal emerges because resource creation begins scaling faster than institutional integration.

The scarcity of funding declines.

The scarcity of deployment remains.

The bottleneck moves.

Infrastructure Inflation Examples

Infrastructure Inflation occurs when structural capacity expands faster than its long-term utilization requirements.

Unlike Knowledge Inflation, where cognitive output exceeds integration capacity, or Resource Inflation, where financial resources exceed deployment capacity, Infrastructure Inflation emerges when physical, organizational, institutional, or technological structures continue to accumulate despite future demand being uncertain, delayed, or changing.

Historically, infrastructure is constructed in anticipation of future needs.

Roads are built before traffic arrives.

Factories are built before production begins.

Datacenters are built before models are trained.

Office buildings are constructed before companies occupy them.

Such anticipation is not a flaw.

In many cases it is necessary.

Infrastructure often requires far longer development cycles than either knowledge generation or resource allocation. Because of these delays, societies routinely construct capacity based upon expectations regarding future growth.

The result creates a distinctive vulnerability.

If future demand evolves differently than anticipated, infrastructure may remain technically functional, economically useful, and physically intact, while simultaneously becoming structurally overabundant.

In triadic terms:

Infrastructure ≫ Future Utilization

The following examples illustrate different manifestations of this process.

Office Real Estate After COVID-19

One of the clearest recent examples of Infrastructure Inflation emerged within commercial office real estate following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Prior to the pandemic, prevailing economic trends encouraged the construction of large quantities of office infrastructure. Growing service sectors, expanding corporate employment, urban concentration, and expectations of continued economic growth all supported increasing demand for office space.

The resulting investments appeared rational.

Companies required offices.

Workers commuted daily.

Commercial districts expanded.

New office developments generated attractive financial returns.

The relationship between utilization and infrastructure remained broadly aligned.

The pandemic disrupted this alignment.

Remote work, hybrid work models, distributed teams, and new collaboration technologies altered how organizations utilized physical space.

Many employees demonstrated a preference for flexible work arrangements.

Many organizations discovered that at least part of their operations could continue effectively without requiring full-time office presence.

Importantly, the underlying infrastructure did not disappear.

The buildings remained.

The utilities remained.

The leases remained.

The financing structures remained.

Yet utilization changed dramatically.

As a consequence, many regions experienced rising vacancy rates, declining commercial property valuations, and increasing financial pressure on office real estate markets.

Viewed through the lens of Triadic Inflation, this episode does not imply that the original investments were irrational.

Rather, infrastructure constructed under one set of expectations became partially decoupled from future utilization requirements.

The resulting imbalance represents a particularly clear example of Infrastructure Inflation.

Cryptocurrency Mining Infrastructure

Cryptocurrency mining provides a technological example of the same phenomenon.

As cryptocurrency valuations increased, large amounts of capital were reinvested into mining infrastructure, including specialized hardware, cooling systems, energy contracts, datacenters, and operational ecosystems.

As long as mining activity remained economically attractive, continued infrastructure expansion appeared justified.

However, changes in technology and network design can alter infrastructural requirements abruptly.

A particularly illustrative example emerged following Ethereum’s transition away from Proof-of-Work mining.

Large quantities of mining infrastructure remained physically operational, yet much of the demand that originally justified its construction disappeared.

The infrastructure itself retained value.

The economic environment surrounding it had changed.

Infrastructure growth had outpaced future utilization.

Once again:

Infrastructure ≫ Future Utilization

The phenomenon therefore illustrates how infrastructure inflation can emerge even when the underlying technology itself remains successful.

AI Datacenter Expansion

Contemporary AI ecosystems may represent the largest ongoing infrastructure expansion of the present decade.

Enormous investments are currently being directed toward datacenters, compute clusters, networking infrastructure, cooling systems, energy generation, semiconductor manufacturing, and related supply chains.

Much of this construction is justified by expectations regarding future AI demand and future computational requirements.

Such expectations may ultimately prove correct.

Indeed, future AI systems may require substantially more infrastructure than exists today.

However, the history of technological development suggests that demand is not the only variable that determines infrastructure requirements.

Efficiency matters as well.

Algorithmic improvements, hardware advances, architectural innovations, and entirely new computational paradigms can dramatically reduce the amount of infrastructure required to deliver a given level of capability.

As a result, successful technological progress can sometimes reduce the future utilization of existing infrastructure.

This possibility distinguishes Infrastructure Inflation from simple speculative excess.

The underlying technology may continue improving.

The demand may remain real.

Yet previously constructed capacity may still become partially overabundant if future efficiency gains outpace infrastructure usage.

From the perspective of Triadic Inflation, the critical issue therefore is not whether AI succeeds.

The critical issue is whether future utilization evolves at the same rate as infrastructure expansion.

History suggests that these two variables do not always remain aligned.

Common Pattern

Viewed collectively, office real estate, cryptocurrency mining, and AI datacenter expansion illustrate a common structural pattern.

In all three cases, infrastructure is constructed based on reasonable expectations regarding future demand.

The resulting investments may be rational.

The infrastructure itself may be functional.

The underlying technologies may remain valuable.

Yet changes in utilization patterns, economic conditions, social behavior, or technological efficiency can cause infrastructure growth to exceed future structural requirements.

Infrastructure Inflation therefore does not imply failure.

It implies misalignment.

The scarcity of capacity declines.

The scarcity of utilization remains.

The bottleneck moves.

Implications for AI, Science and Economic Systems

The examples presented throughout this paper suggest that Triadic Inflation should not be interpreted as a phenomenon restricted to a single industry or technology.

Rather, it appears whenever recursive systems generate asymmetric growth among Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

Consequently, the framework has implications that extend beyond artificial intelligence and apply more generally to scientific, economic, institutional, and technological systems.

A common assumption in modern technological discourse is that additional resources inevitably accelerate progress.

More capital funds more research.

More infrastructure enables larger experiments.

More compute enables larger models.

While this reasoning is often correct, the examples considered in this paper suggest that the relationship is neither linear nor guaranteed.

The cryptocurrency example demonstrates that infrastructure can expand faster than future utilization.

The cybersecurity example demonstrates that knowledge can expand faster than remediation capacity.

The Riemann Hypothesis example demonstrates that proof production can expand faster than interpretation and integration.

The Anthropic example demonstrates that resources can expand based upon anticipated future capabilities that have not yet been fully absorbed by surrounding institutions and markets.

Triadic Inflation therefore suggests that progress within one dimension does not automatically remove bottlenecks within the other two.

Perhaps the most important implication concerns the role of knowledge itself.

Many contemporary discussions surrounding artificial intelligence assume that sufficient compute, investment, and scale inevitably translate into the discovery of missing knowledge.

Yet history provides many examples where this does not appear to be the case.

Fusion research offers an interesting illustration.

For decades, the field has benefited from substantial scientific attention, large-scale institutional support, significant financial investment, and increasingly sophisticated experimental infrastructure. Despite numerous breakthroughs and incremental advances, a widely adopted commercial fusion energy system remains absent.

Whether this reflects a remaining engineering challenge, an unresolved scientific problem, or a deeper structural limitation remains debated. The important observation for the present paper is simply that resources and infrastructure alone have not guaranteed the appearance of the missing knowledge required to close the system.

This example highlights an important asymmetry.

Some domains may experience Knowledge Inflation.

Other domains may experience Knowledge Scarcity.

Both situations remain fully compatible with the triadic framework.

The critical issue is not absolute abundance or scarcity, but imbalance between the three dimensions.

The framework also suggests a reinterpretation of many contemporary AI debates.

Discussions surrounding Artificial General Intelligence frequently focus on capabilities.

Can a system reason?

Can a system discover mathematics?

Can a system automate research?

Can a system generate software?

These questions are important, but they address only one component of the closure.

Even dramatic increases in capability do not automatically resolve questions of integration.

Educational systems must adapt.

Organizations must adapt.

Regulatory systems must adapt.

Verification systems must adapt.

Labor markets must adapt.

Consequently, increasing intelligence does not eliminate structural constraints. It can instead make those constraints more visible.

This observation may help explain why rapid technological progress often coexists with persistent societal bottlenecks.

The limiting factor gradually shifts.

In pre-industrial societies, energy and material resources were often the dominant constraints.

In modern scientific societies, infrastructure and coordination become increasingly important.

In highly automated environments, knowledge production itself may become abundant, shifting scarcity toward interpretation, validation, attention, governance, and closure maintenance.

The bottleneck does not disappear.

It migrates.

Viewed through this lens, many contemporary concerns about AI may be misclassified.

The central challenge is not necessarily runaway intelligence, runaway investment, or runaway infrastructure in isolation.

The challenge is maintaining balance between the three dimensions as each continues to evolve through recursive feedback.

The resulting perspective differs substantially from narratives based solely on technological optimism or technological pessimism.

A triadic system may become unstable despite genuine progress.

A triadic system may remain stable despite speculative excess.

The determining factor is not the magnitude of growth but the degree of structural alignment within the larger closure.

The broader implication is therefore straightforward.

Future scientific and technological progress may increasingly depend not on maximizing Knowledge, Resources, or Infrastructure individually, but on preserving their balance.

When one dimension significantly outpaces the other two, inflation, temporal misalignment, and overshoot emerge.

When the dimensions remain sufficiently aligned, recursive growth can increase the carrying capacity of the system itself.

In this sense, Triadic Inflation provides not merely a description of specific contemporary phenomena, but a general framework for analyzing how advanced technological societies evolve under conditions of rapidly accelerating knowledge production.

Collective Attention as an Inflation Signal

The previous sections have described Triadic Inflation as a structural imbalance between Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

A practical challenge immediately follows.

While inflationary phenomena may be observable qualitatively, measuring the underlying imbalance directly is often difficult. Knowledge generation, capital formation, infrastructure construction, institutional adaptation, educational capacity, and technological integration evolve through complex interacting processes that are rarely visible in real time.

For this reason, the present paper proposes a complementary heuristic indicator based on an increasingly important feature of modern information societies: collective attention.

The central observation is simple.

When one dimension of a triadic system begins expanding substantially faster than the others, collective attention often becomes disproportionately concentrated on that dimension.

This attention may emerge through academic publications, traditional media, policy discussions, public debate, online communities, social media platforms, podcasts, newsletters, livestreams, blogs, content creators, and independent researchers.

The phenomenon is not restricted to professional journalism.

Indeed, in many contemporary technological ecosystems, public attention may become visible first through decentralized information networks before appearing in traditional institutions.

Consequently, collective attention can serve as a practical signal of where society perceives opportunities, risks, uncertainty, scarcity, or structural tension.

The attention itself does not constitute inflation.

Rather, it may reveal where inflationary pressures are becoming visible.

Historically, prices often served as the primary observable indicator of scarcity within economic systems.

Rising prices signaled increasing competition for limited resources.

Modern information societies contain an additional layer.

Attention itself has become a scarce resource.

Individuals can only read a limited number of papers.

Organizations can only track a limited number of developments.

Researchers can only evaluate a limited number of results.

Citizens can only follow a limited number of public debates.

As a result, attention increasingly behaves as a signaling mechanism for structural importance.

While prices often reveal economic scarcity, collective attention may reveal structural scarcity.

An important aspect of this observation is that attention does not necessarily distribute itself evenly across a triadic system.

Instead, it often becomes concentrated around the dimension currently experiencing the greatest inflationary pressure.

The resulting concentration itself may function as an observable symptom of imbalance.

Knowledge Inflation and Attention Concentration

Knowledge Inflation frequently manifests through public discussion focused on cognitive production.

Topics include scientific breakthroughs, mathematical discoveries, artificial intelligence capabilities, cybersecurity findings, software automation, technological unemployment, educational disruption, existential-risk discussions, and other forms of accelerated knowledge generation.

In such periods, collective attention becomes concentrated on the creation of knowledge itself.

Researchers debate breakthroughs.

Experts debate implications.

Influencers and content creators discuss the latest discoveries.

Podcasters interview specialists.

Social-media discussions focus on emerging capabilities and their consequences.

Importantly, the surrounding Resource and Infrastructure dimensions may receive comparatively less attention even though they remain essential components of the larger system.

The concentration of attention therefore reflects the location of the perceived bottleneck.

Resource Inflation and Attention Concentration

Resource Inflation exhibits a different pattern.

Public attention increasingly shifts toward capital allocation, valuations, mergers, funding rounds, investment programs, market capitalization, speculative opportunities, and financial expectations.

Discussion becomes dominated by questions such as:

  • Which company is worth the most?

  • Which startup received the largest investment?

  • Which industry attracts the most capital?

  • Which technology could generate the largest future returns?

The attention no longer focuses primarily on scientific discoveries or technical infrastructure.

Instead, it focuses on the movement and concentration of resources.

Future possibilities become translated into present economic narratives.

The resulting discourse often revolves around financial scale rather than scientific or infrastructural details.

Infrastructure Inflation and Attention Concentration

Infrastructure Inflation generates a third attention pattern.

Discussion increasingly focuses on datacenters, electrical grids, semiconductor production, housing markets, transportation systems, office real estate, supply chains, physical capacity constraints, or other forms of structural support.

Questions typically concern capacity rather than discovery or valuation.

Can the electrical grid support future demand?

Can sufficient datacenters be constructed?

Can transportation networks absorb additional activity?

Can office capacity be utilized effectively?

Once again, attention becomes concentrated around a specific component of the triadic closure.

The underlying concern is no longer knowledge generation or capital formation.

The concern becomes structural capacity itself.

Artificial Intelligence as a Multi-Dimensional Example

Artificial intelligence provides a particularly revealing example because the same technological phenomenon generates attention patterns across all three dimensions.

When public discourse focuses on AI-generated scientific discoveries, software generation, cybersecurity, mathematical proofs, technological unemployment, or existential-risk discussions, collective attention is primarily concentrated on the Knowledge dimension.

When discourse focuses on company valuations, investment rounds, acquisitions, venture capital activity, future profitability, or financial expectations, attention shifts toward the Resource dimension.

When discourse focuses on datacenters, electricity consumption, semiconductor manufacturing, cooling systems, power generation, or compute capacity, attention shifts toward the Infrastructure dimension.

The underlying technology remains identical.

The narrative changes.

What shifts is not the object of analysis but the location where society perceives the greatest pressure, uncertainty, opportunity, or emerging bottleneck.

This observation is particularly significant because the three narratives often remain surprisingly disconnected from one another.

Discussions surrounding frontier AI valuations rarely focus on specific research papers.

Discussions surrounding mathematical breakthroughs rarely focus on capital allocation.

Discussions surrounding datacenters rarely focus on theorem generation or company valuations.

Instead, collective attention tends to concentrate on a single dimension at a time.

The resulting asymmetry may itself provide information regarding where inflationary pressures are becoming most visible.

The Attention Economy as a Distributed Sensor Network

The emergence of social media, independent creators, livestreams, podcasts, blogs, newsletters, and online communities introduces an additional aspect to this interpretation.

Many modern information producers operate within attention-driven economies.

Content creators, analysts, influencers, researchers, and commentators often earn income by identifying topics that attract interest, engagement, uncertainty, controversy, or discussion.

This economic incentive does not necessarily invalidate the signal.

On the contrary, it may amplify it.

Large numbers of independent participants continuously search for subjects that capture public attention.

Collectively, they behave as a distributed sensing network monitoring societal interests and perceived bottlenecks.

As inflationary pressures emerge within one dimension of a triadic system, content production frequently becomes concentrated around that dimension.

Videos, articles, commentaries, livestreams, podcasts, and discussions begin addressing similar themes from different perspectives.

A growing fraction of collective attention is drawn toward the same underlying source of tension.

The present paper does not propose collective attention as a rigorous quantitative measure of Triadic Inflation.

Rather, it is proposed as a practical societal indicator.

A sudden concentration of public, institutional, academic, media, and social-media attention around a particular dimension of a triadic system may signal that growth within that dimension is beginning to outpace the adaptive capacity of the others.

In this sense, collective attention functions as a modern observational proxy for inflationary pressure.

As scarcity migrates, attention tends to migrate with it.

The dominant narratives of a technological era may therefore provide an early indication of where structural imbalances are emerging before their consequences become fully visible through economic, scientific, or institutional outcomes.

The Emergence of Attention as a Modern Signal

An important limitation of the proposed attention-based indicator is that it should not be interpreted as a timeless measurement mechanism.

Triadic Inflation itself may be considerably older than the information systems capable of making it visible.

Historical examples such as railway expansion, electrification, industrialization, automotive adoption, telecommunications development, or earlier speculative investment cycles likely exhibited many of the same underlying dynamics described throughout this paper.

Knowledge expanded.

Resources accumulated.

Infrastructure was constructed.

Bottlenecks migrated.

Structural imbalances emerged.

What differed was the visibility of those processes.

Prior to the emergence of large-scale digital communication networks, collective attention was difficult to observe directly.

Public discourse was primarily mediated through newspapers, magazines, professional organizations, academic institutions, and political structures. Information propagation was comparatively slow, and the ability to monitor large-scale shifts in collective attention was limited.

The nineteenth-century railway expansions provide an illustrative example.

Although newspapers certainly reported on railway construction, investment activity, and associated economic developments, the resulting attention signals remained fragmented, localized, and difficult to measure systematically.

A modern observer had no equivalent of social-media platforms, search trends, online discussion forums, video platforms, livestreams, engagement metrics, or large-scale digital archives capable of revealing where attention was becoming concentrated in near real time.

Consequently, historical societies may have experienced Triadic Inflation without possessing a practical mechanism for observing the migration of collective attention associated with it.

The situation is fundamentally different within contemporary information economies.

The rise of the internet, social media, digital analytics, content platforms, recommendation systems, podcasts, independent blogs, livestreaming, and creator-driven ecosystems has transformed attention into an increasingly observable quantity.

Large populations continuously reveal collective interests through search behavior, viewing behavior, subscriptions, shares, comments, engagement patterns, and content creation.

For perhaps the first time in history, societal attention leaves a persistent and measurable digital trace.

This development is particularly visible among younger generations whose information consumption is increasingly decentralized.

Many individuals now encounter technological, economic, scientific, and political developments first through content creators, influencers, podcasters, independent journalists, researchers, newsletter authors, and online communities rather than through traditional media organizations.

Such actors frequently compete for attention and therefore possess strong incentives to identify emerging topics before they become widely recognized by established institutions.

As a result, attention concentrations often appear first within distributed online ecosystems and only later within traditional media and academic discourse.

From the perspective of Triadic Inflation, this creates a novel opportunity.

The migration of bottlenecks between Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure may now become visible through corresponding migrations of collective attention.

Rather than waiting for years of economic data, institutional reports, or historical analysis, observers can often witness emerging imbalances through shifts in public discourse itself.

Attention therefore functions as a uniquely modern observational layer superimposed upon a much older structural phenomenon.

Triadic Inflation may not be new.

The ability to observe it through the collective dynamics of the attention economy may be.

Boundary Conditions and Non-Examples

Like any conceptual framework, Triadic Inflation should not be interpreted as a universal explanation for all forms of economic, scientific, technological, or societal change.

The framework is specifically intended to describe situations in which observable inflationary phenomena emerge from structural imbalances within recursive Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure cycles.

Not every form of disruption, resistance, conflict, or instability satisfies these conditions.

The following examples illustrate important boundary conditions and help clarify where the framework may be less applicable.

Transmission Acceleration without Inflation

An important distinction exists between the accelerated production of knowledge and the accelerated transmission of knowledge.

The invention of the printing press provides an illustrative example.

The printing press dramatically increased the efficiency with which existing knowledge could be copied, distributed, preserved, and shared.

Books became more accessible.

Literacy expanded.

Ideas spread more rapidly across geographical and political boundaries.

However, the primary effect was not necessarily an explosive increase in knowledge production itself.

Rather, the innovation expanded the capacity of the surrounding infrastructure responsible for transmitting knowledge.

In triadic terms, the printing press primarily strengthened the link between Knowledge and Infrastructure rather than creating a persistent imbalance between them.

The resulting effect reduced communication bottlenecks and improved knowledge accessibility.

While institutional resistance certainly emerged, particularly from organizations whose authority depended upon controlling information, the dominant dynamic was not one of knowledge abundance overwhelming integration capacity.

Instead, transmission capacity increased.

Consequently, the printing press should not automatically be interpreted as an example of Triadic Inflation.

It may instead represent a case where infrastructure adaptation successfully expanded alongside knowledge transmission.

Cultural and Ideological Resistance

Triadic Inflation focuses on structural bottlenecks generated by asymmetric growth rates within recursive systems.

Not all forms of societal resistance arise from such mechanisms.

Religious conflicts, ideological disputes, cultural disagreements, ethical controversies, and value-based social movements often emerge from competing belief systems rather than from imbalances between Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

A society may possess sufficient knowledge, sufficient resources, and sufficient infrastructure to adopt a particular innovation while still rejecting that innovation on moral, cultural, or ideological grounds.

The resulting resistance is therefore driven primarily by differences in values rather than limitations in adaptive capacity.

Although such conflicts may coexist with triadic inflationary dynamics, the framework should not be interpreted as a complete explanation for them.

The principal bottleneck in these cases is normative rather than structural.

Military Competition and Strategic Conflict

A further limitation arises in domains dominated by strategic competition rather than productive integration.

Military systems often prioritize deterrence, security, survivability, redundancy, and geopolitical advantage rather than economic efficiency.

Under such conditions, infrastructure may intentionally exceed immediate utilization requirements.

Redundant production capacity, reserve forces, strategic stockpiles, and defensive systems may all appear inefficient from a purely economic perspective while remaining strategically rational.

Similarly, technological development during periods of conflict can be driven more by adversarial dynamics than by recursive closure optimization.

Although Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure remain important, the dominant behavior of the system is often governed by strategic interactions between competing actors rather than by bottleneck migration within a single adaptive closure.

The explanatory power of Triadic Inflation is therefore reduced in contexts where competition, deterrence, or conflict become the primary drivers of system behavior.

Local Scarcity versus Structural Scarcity

The framework also distinguishes between local shortages and systemic imbalances.

Temporary supply disruptions, natural disasters, political shocks, or isolated market failures may generate short-term scarcity without necessarily reflecting an underlying triadic imbalance.

For example, a sudden shortage of a critical material may temporarily increase prices despite no significant change in Knowledge, Resources, or Infrastructure dynamics.

Such events can be economically important while remaining outside the scope of Triadic Inflation.

The framework is concerned primarily with persistent structural patterns that emerge from recursive growth processes rather than with short-lived disruptions.

Interpretation

These limitations clarify an important aspect of the present work.

Triadic Inflation should not be viewed as a universal theory of all forms of change, instability, or scarcity.

Instead, it is best understood as a framework for identifying situations in which rapid expansion of one dimension within a recursive Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure closure causes scarcity to migrate toward the slower-adapting dimensions.

When such migration occurs, inflationary effects become visible.

When bottlenecks arise primarily from cultural conflict, strategic competition, value systems, or isolated external shocks, alternative frameworks may provide more appropriate explanations.

The framework therefore gains explanatory power not by explaining everything, but by identifying a specific class of structural imbalances that recur across technological, scientific, and economic systems.

Conclusion

Classical inflation is traditionally associated with money, while classical bubble theory focuses primarily on valuation.

This paper has argued that a broader class of inflationary phenomena emerges naturally within recursive triadic systems.

Within the Knowledge–Resource–Infrastructure framework, inflation is not restricted to financial assets. Instead, inflation occurs whenever one dimension of a recursive closure expands substantially faster than the remaining dimensions can absorb, integrate, or support.

The resulting imbalance reduces the relative scarcity and significance of individual units produced by the expanding dimension.

Knowledge becomes abundant relative to interpretation.

Resources become abundant relative to deployment.

Infrastructure becomes abundant relative to utilization.

Inflation therefore appears not as a purely monetary phenomenon but as a general manifestation of structural asymmetry.

The contemporary AI ecosystem provides unusually clear examples of this process.

The Anthropic case illustrates Resource Inflation, where future capabilities become capitalized into present resources.

The Riemann Hypothesis episode illustrates Knowledge Inflation, where proof generation and formal verification potentially accelerate faster than human understanding and institutional integration.

Cybersecurity illustrates how vulnerability discovery can outpace remediation capacity, creating Infrastructure Stress despite increasing knowledge.

Cryptocurrency mining illustrates Infrastructure Inflation, where physical and computational capacity may expand beyond future structural requirements.

Although differing in detail, each example exhibits the same underlying pattern: recursive growth occurring faster than closure adaptation.

The resulting perspective differs significantly from conventional bubble narratives.

A bubble is often understood as an excess of expectation over reality.

Triadic Inflation proposes a different possibility.

Reality itself may accelerate.

The technology may function.

The discoveries may be genuine.

The infrastructure may exist.

The resources may be available.

Yet instability may still emerge because the dimensions of the closure no longer evolve at compatible rates.

Inflation therefore becomes an observable symptom of deeper structural imbalance rather than the primary cause of instability.

This interpretation naturally connects the present work to the broader Triadic Cosmos ecosystem.

The Closure Overshoot Law explains how recursive systems exceed their carrying capacity.

Temporal Misalignment explains how closure components evolve on incompatible timescales.

Triadic Inflation explains how these processes become visible through changing scarcity structures within Knowledge, Resources, and Infrastructure.

The three frameworks therefore describe different aspects of the same underlying phenomenon.

Perhaps the most important implication is that future technological progress may increasingly shift bottlenecks rather than eliminate them.

The scarcity of code may be replaced by the scarcity of maintenance.

The scarcity of proofs may be replaced by the scarcity of interpretation.

The scarcity of vulnerabilities may be replaced by the scarcity of remediation.

The scarcity of infrastructure may be replaced by the scarcity of utilization.

The bottleneck migrates.

Viewed through this lens, the central challenge facing advanced technological societies is not simply how to maximize knowledge, resources, or infrastructure individually.

The challenge is preserving structural balance among them.

When that balance is maintained, recursive growth increases the carrying capacity of the entire system.

When it is not, inflation, temporal misalignment, and overshoot emerge as inevitable consequences of the same recursive dynamics.

In this sense, Triadic Inflation provides a general framework for understanding how accelerating systems evolve, where their bottlenecks appear, and why successful growth can produce instability even when the underlying progress is entirely real.
