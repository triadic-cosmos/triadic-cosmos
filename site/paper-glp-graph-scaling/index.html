Significance Statement

The GLP–Graph (GLPG) architecture was originally introduced as a minimal and interpretable cognitive substrate capable of combining deterministic graph routing with emergent semantic manifolds. The present paper extends this foundation by demonstrating that GLPG is not merely a static architecture, but a scalable and evolution-capable cognitive system whose attractor geometry remains stable across architectural transformations, dataset transfers, and staged generational growth.

This work introduces the first numerical evaluation pipeline for GLPG: all generated stories across all scaling experiments are assessed by an external LLM using a calibrated scoring regime. This provides an objective and reproducible metric for comparing cognitive behaviour across model sizes, generative modes, training depths, staged lineages, and transfer-learning directions. The resulting measurements reveal that GLPG supports function-preserving scaling: the learned lemma embeddings and paging geometry act as cognitive DNA that survives both upscaling and downscaling, enabling models to grow or shrink without cognitive reset.

The experiments show that GLPG exhibits unusually strong structural and semantic stability. Upscaling inflates the semantic manifold while preserving canonical structure; downscaling compresses the manifold without erasing core attractors; staged training produces hereditary semantic clusters that propagate across generations; and transfer learning reorganises the manifold in a deterministic, canon-dominant manner. Large models trained from scratch perform significantly worse than lineage-grown models, demonstrating that capacity alone is insufficient without prior consolidation at smaller scales.

These findings position GLPG as a minimal yet robust cognitive substrate. Its bidirectional scalability, hereditary attractor dynamics, stability under variation, and staged growth behaviour provide a concrete computational analogue for cognitive development, inheritance, and world-formation within the Triadic Cosmos ecosystem. GLPG therefore serves not only as a language generator, but as a structurally grounded model of scalable artificial cognition.

Introduction

The GLP–Graph (GLPG) architecture was introduced as a hybrid generative substrate that unifies deterministic graph routing with emergent semantic behaviour inside a single monolithic MLP. Whereas conventional language models rely on large-scale attention mechanisms and implicit statistical correlations, GLPG provides an explicit and inspectable governance layer: grammar tokens, lemma transitions, paging geometry, and structural cadence are defined by a canonical graph, while semantic content is injected through learned lemma embeddings and a compact feedforward network. This combination yields a system that is both structurally deterministic and semantically expressive, forming a minimal cognitive substrate within the Triadic Cosmos ecosystem.

Previous work established two foundational properties of GLPG. The architecture paper demonstrated that GLPG supports dual generative modes—paged graph traversal and pure MLP sampling—revealing that the monolithic network internalises manifold structure that mirrors the deterministic graph. The hyperparameter study showed that GLPG exhibits structured cognitive dynamics across scale: proto-semantic attractors in underscaled models, emergent semantic manifolds in mid-scale configurations, and canon compression in larger models. These behaviours suggest that GLPG operates not merely as a language generator, but as a low-dimensional dynamical system whose attractor geometry evolves predictably with capacity.

The present paper extends this line of inquiry by investigating GLPG under architectural scaling, staged generational growth, and cross-dataset transfer. We examine how the attractor manifold behaves when the hidden-layer capacity of the monolithic MLP is increased, decreased, or grown through lineage. Crucially, we introduce the first numerical evaluation pipeline for GLPG: all generated stories across all scaling experiments are assessed by an external LLM using a calibrated scoring regime. This provides an objective and reproducible metric for comparing cognitive behaviour across model sizes, generative modes, training depths, staged lineages, and transfer- learning directions.

Using this metric, we show that GLPG supports function-preserving scaling. The learned lemma embeddings and paging geometry act as cognitive DNA that remains stable across architectural transformations. Upscaling expands the semantic manifold without erasing existing attractors; downscaling compresses the manifold while preserving canonical structure; staged training produces hereditary attractor clusters that propagate across generations; and transfer learning reorganises the manifold in a deterministic, canon-dominant manner. Large models trained from scratch perform significantly worse than lineage-grown models, demonstrating that capacity alone is insufficient without prior consolidation.

Across all experiments, GLPG exhibits unusually strong structural and semantic stability. Its manifold deforms smoothly under hyperparameter variation, scaling, compression, and dataset transfer, behaving not as a statistical correlation space but as a structured cognitive dynamical system. This stability enables predictable cognitive development across architectural stages and provides a foundation for lineage-based world formation.

Overall, this paper positions GLPG as a scalable and evolution-capable cognitive substrate. Its bidirectional stability, hereditary attractor dynamics, transfer-learning behaviour, and staged growth properties provide a computational analogue for the AGI lifecycle concept: cognitive systems that develop, compress, inherit, and expand their internal world models across generations.

Related Work

Research on scaling behaviour in neural architectures has largely focused on transformers and their predecessors—MLPs, RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, and convolutional sequence models. Although these systems have achieved impressive empirical performance, none exhibit the combination of function-preserving scaling, attractor stability, staged consolidation, and lineage formation demonstrated by GLPG.

Scaling in Conventional Neural Architectures

Early neural language models (MLPs, RNNs, LSTMs) do not support architectural scaling without full retraining. Increasing hidden-layer capacity changes the model’s inductive biases, alters its internal attractor landscape, and typically requires training from scratch. Downscaling is even more destructive: reducing capacity collapses attractors, erases learned structure, and produces models that bear little resemblance to their larger predecessors. No lineage or inheritance is possible.

Transformers introduced more predictable scaling laws, but only in the statistical sense. Kaplan et al. (2020) and subsequent work showed that transformer performance follows power-law relationships with respect to dataset size, model size, and compute. However, these laws do not imply function-preserving scaling. A larger transformer is not a continuation of a smaller one: it must be trained from scratch, and its internal representations differ substantially. Attempts at function-preserving scaling—such as Net2Net transformations, layer expansion, or width doubling—have limited applicability and do not preserve semantic manifolds in generative models.

Moreover, transformers exhibit several instability modes under scaling: attention collapse, gradient interference, catastrophic forgetting, embedding drift, and sensitivity to initialization and optimizer dynamics. Small changes in hyperparameters or dataset composition can produce large, unpredictable shifts in output quality. These behaviours contrast sharply with GLPG’s smooth manifold deformation under upscaling, downscaling, and staged growth.

Distillation, Compression, and Model Surgery

Knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) compresses large models into smaller ones, but the resulting student model is not a structural continuation of the teacher. Distillation is lossy, requires a separate training process, and does not preserve the teacher’s attractor geometry. Similarly, pruning, quantization, and low-rank factorization reduce model size but degrade semantic structure and do not support reversible scaling.

Net2Net transformations (Chen et al., 2016) introduced function-preserving network expansion and contraction for feedforward models, but these techniques do not apply to generative architectures with recurrent or attention-based dynamics. Even when applicable, Net2Net transformations preserve only local function approximations, not global attractor manifolds or semantic world models.

GLPG differs fundamentally: downscaling acts as self-distillation, compressing the manifold without erasing canonical attractors, while upscaling expands capacity without cognitive reset. This bidirectional, reversible scaling has no analogue in existing language model architectures.

Staged Growth and Lineage Formation

Staged or curriculum-based training has been explored in various contexts, including progressive nets, growing neural gas, and curriculum learning. However, these methods do not produce hereditary attractor basins or multi-generational cognitive lineages. Transformers trained with curricula do not inherit structured attractors from smaller ancestors; they simply converge more quickly or more stably.

The closest analogue is the Toy Academy work, which demonstrated staged growth for function learning. GLPG extends this idea from functions to worlds: staged training produces semantic clusters that propagate across generations, enabling genuine lineage formation. No existing language model architecture exhibits this behaviour.

Transfer Learning and Canon Dynamics

Transfer learning in transformers typically results in catastrophic forgetting or unstable interference between domains. Multi-domain training requires careful balancing, adapter layers, or mixture-of-experts routing to avoid collapse. Even then, semantic interference remains a major challenge.

GLPG’s transfer learning behaviour is qualitatively different. The attractor manifold reorganises in a deterministic, canon-dominant manner: vocabulary persists, but semantic activation follows the dataset with the strongest attractor structure. This is not catastrophic forgetting but a stable, predictable cognitive overwrite analogous to biological learning dynamics.

Summary

Across all prior architectures, scaling is either destructive (downscaling), non-preserving (upscaling), or requires full retraining (transformers). No existing system supports reversible, function-preserving scaling; no system maintains stable attractor geometry across architectural transformations; no system exhibits hereditary semantic clusters; and no system demonstrates canon-dominant transfer learning.

GLPG therefore occupies a unique position in the landscape of neural architectures: a minimal, interpretable, evolution-capable cognitive substrate whose scaling behaviour has no precedent in conventional language models.

Effects of Grammar Fixing

The original GLPG engine included an optional grammar–fixing layer designed to correct surface-level linguistic mistakes after generation. Although well-intentioned, this mechanism introduced a critical flaw: it operated outside the canonical grammar–lemma graph and was therefore capable of producing tokens that did not exist in the canonical domain. As a result, grammar fixing could break canon, distort lemma embeddings, and introduce semantic drift that did not originate from the GLPG model itself.

Observed Grammar Fix Behaviour

During early experiments, the grammar fixer produced several classes of corrections:

  1. Verb normalization: woulds → would, coulds → could, musts → must, oughts → ought.

  2. Canon-breaking substitutions: These were the most problematic, replacing canonical tokens with unrelated words: Zaidie → aide, murgatroyd → purgatory, dejeuner → Lejeune, Weena → Deena, morlocks → warlocks, saltpetre → saltwater, astronef → estrone. Such substitutions violate the canon-closed nature of GLPG and corrupt the learned lemma geometry.

  3. Contraction repair: did n’t → didn’t, husband ’s → husband’s, and similar apostrophe handling.

  4. Capitalization changes: martian → Martian, mars → Mars, jupiter → Jupiter, andrew → Andrew, mrs. sends → Mrs. Sends.

  5. Singular/plural and conjugation adjustments.

  6. Minor lexical corrections: too → to, a → an, an → a, its → it’s.

  7. Punctuation changes: Adding commas or replacing periods with question marks.

  8. English variant normalization: grey → gray, centre → center, marvellous → marvelous, colour → color, recognise → recognize.

  9. Hyphenation and word-joining: south eastern → south-eastern, any more → anymore.

  10. Insertion of missing verbs or pronouns: e.g. adding is or I.

  11. Word removal: e.g. these their → these.

Although many of these corrections were linguistically reasonable, they were structurally unsafe: the grammar fixer could introduce tokens not present in the canonical dataset, thereby breaking GLPG’s core guarantee of canon-closed generation.

Deterministic Fixing Rules

To eliminate canon-breaking behaviour, the grammar fixer has been replaced by a small set of deterministic, safe post-processing rules. These rules correct only trivial surface-level issues and never introduce new tokens. The current fixing layer applies:

  • contraction normalization: " n’t" → " not", " ’" → "’",

  • simple lexical repairs: " woulds" → " would", " a a" → " an a",

  • specific phrase corrections: "I going" → "I’m going",

  • capitalization of property names (e.g. " america" → " America").

These rules are intentionally minimal and deterministic. They preserve the canonical token inventory, avoid semantic drift, and ensure that the model output is evaluated exactly as generated, without an additional stochastic or corpus-driven correction layer.

Removal of Grammar-Based Postprocessing

The optional grammar checker remains available for diagnostic purposes, but it is no longer used during generation or evaluation. Any discrepancy between the deterministic fixes and the grammar checker output is logged for inspection. This logging mechanism allows detection of potential structural issues without modifying the model output.

Summary

The removal of the original grammar fixer restores GLPG’s core architectural principle: all generated text must remain canon-closed. The new deterministic fixing layer ensures surface-level consistency while preserving the integrity of the learned lemma geometry, the paging structure, and the attractor manifold.

Output Scoring and Evaluation

To compare cognitive behaviour across scaling experiments, every model generates a fixed set of outputs: twenty independent stories of twenty lines each, produced in both generative modes supported by GLPG (paged graph traversal and pure MLP mode). These stories are evaluated by an external LLM, providing an objective and reproducible numerical metric for model quality.

External LLM Evaluator

All evaluations are performed using the Mistral 7B v0.3 instruct model, operating as a blind, architecture-agnostic assessor. The evaluator receives only the generated story text and applies a calibrated scoring regime from 1 to 100. The prompt is:

“Give a score between 1 and 100 for the following short story. The story is written on a moderate level. A score below 50 is perfectly acceptable. Assume the average moderate story scores around 50. Only exceptional stories should score above 80. Evaluate using the following criteria: content, coherence, readability, originality, creativity, style, structure. Do not explain the score.”

This calibrated regime ensures that mid-quality stories cluster around 50, weak stories fall below 40, and only highly coherent or stylistically rich stories exceed 80. The evaluator is invoked twice per story to reduce stochastic variance.

Evaluation Pipeline

The evaluation process follows three steps:

  1. Story extraction. Each output file is segmented into stories of at least twenty lines. Shorter fragments are discarded. A maximum of one thousand stories may be processed, though all experiments in this paper use exactly twenty.

  2. Dual scoring. For each story, the evaluator produces two independent scores:

    s1, s2 ∈ [1,100].

    If either score falls outside the valid range (due to malformed output), the evaluator retries until a valid score is obtained.

  3. Score consolidation. From the two scores, three per-story values are computed:

    $$s_{\min} = \min(s_1, s_2), \qquad s_{\max} = \max(s_1, s_2), \qquad s_{\mathrm{avg}} = \left\lfloor \frac{s_1 + s_2 + 1}{2} \right\rfloor.$$

    These values capture evaluator variance and provide a representative score for the story.

Score Aggregation

To obtain a compact and comparable metric across all scaling experiments, the twenty stories generated by each model are aggregated into six values:

  • Total Min: ∑smin — global lower-bound quality.

  • Total Max: ∑smax — global upper-bound quality.

  • Total Avg: ∑savg — representative global quality.

  • Min: min savg — weakest attractor basin.

  • Max: max savg — strongest attractor basin.

  • Median: median of all savg — typical attractor strength.

These six values form a stable cognitive fingerprint of each model. The total scores measure global attractor strength and semantic coherence; the per-story min, max, and median measure manifold spread, attractor depth, and narrative stability. Together, they allow direct comparison between models of different sizes, training depths, and generational lineages.

Rationale

GLPG is a deterministic-structural yet semantically emergent architecture. Its behaviour is best captured not by classical statistical metrics, but by measures that reflect attractor geometry, semantic basin depth, and manifold spread. The six aggregated values introduced here provide a compact and interpretable representation of these cognitive properties, enabling quantitative analysis of scaling behaviour across all experiments in this paper.

Comparison with Conventional Language Model Evaluation

Conventional language models are typically evaluated using statistical benchmarks such as perplexity, next-token prediction accuracy, masked-token recovery, or large-scale QA and reasoning datasets. These metrics assume a probabilistic generative mechanism in which the model attempts to approximate the statistical distribution of a corpus. Such evaluations are appropriate for transformer-based architectures, where semantic behaviour emerges from high-dimensional attention patterns and large-scale correlation.

GLPG does not operate in this regime. Its generative behaviour is governed by a deterministic grammar–lemma graph, explicit paging geometry, and a compact monolithic MLP that injects semantic content into a canon-closed structural substrate. As a result, GLPG is not a statistical language model: it does not attempt to predict corpus-level token frequencies, nor does it optimize likelihood over a distribution. Instead, GLPG produces structured prose constrained by a fixed canonical domain. The quality of its output can therefore never exceed the quality of the canonical dataset itself; the dataset defines an upper bound on semantic richness, stylistic variation, and narrative complexity.

Because GLPG is canon-driven rather than distribution-driven, conventional LM benchmarks provide little insight into its cognitive behaviour. Perplexity cannot be meaningfully computed on a model whose transitions are filtered by deterministic grammar rules and paging constraints. Masked-token recovery is incompatible with GLPG’s explicit routing and lemma geometry. Reasoning and QA benchmarks assume open-domain semantic generalization, whereas GLPG operates within a closed canonical world.

For these reasons, the present paper adopts an external LLM-based evaluation method rather than statistical LM metrics. The numerical scoring regime introduced here captures the aspects of GLPG that matter: attractor stability, manifold coherence, narrative flow, and stylistic consistency. These properties reflect GLPG’s architectural identity as a deterministic cognitive substrate rather than a probabilistic language model. The evaluation method therefore aligns with GLPG’s design principles and provides a meaningful basis for comparing scaling behaviour across models and generations.

Comparison with Human Evaluation

Human linguistic ability is traditionally assessed not through statistical benchmarks, but through qualitative scoring of written output. In educational and linguistic settings, a short essay or composition is evaluated by another human using criteria such as content, coherence, readability, creativity, style, and structural clarity. These criteria closely mirror the dimensions used in the LLM-based evaluator adopted in this paper. Human language competence is therefore measured through holistic judgement rather than through token-level prediction accuracy or probabilistic metrics.

Conventional language-model benchmarks do not align with this human-centric evaluation framework. Perplexity assumes that a model should approximate corpus-level token frequencies; QA benchmarks assume broad factual knowledge and open-domain reasoning; masked-token recovery assumes statistical reconstruction of missing information. None of these assumptions apply to human language production, where quality is judged by narrative flow, stylistic consistency, and conceptual coherence rather than by statistical likelihood.

GLPG operates in a similar regime. As a canon-driven architecture, GLPG generates text within a closed structural and semantic domain defined by its canonical dataset. The quality of its output can therefore never exceed the quality of the canon itself; the dataset imposes an upper bound on semantic richness and stylistic variation. This mirrors human writing: a person cannot produce knowledge or stylistic nuance beyond what they have been exposed to. GLPG’s generative behaviour is thus constrained by its learned world model rather than by open-domain statistical generalization.

For these reasons, human-style evaluation is more appropriate for GLPG than conventional LM benchmarks. The external LLM assessor used in this paper functions analogously to a human grader: it reads the generated story, applies qualitative criteria, and assigns a holistic score. This approach captures the aspects of GLPG that matter—attractor stability, narrative coherence, semantic consistency, and stylistic fidelity—while avoiding metrics that presuppose statistical language modelling.

In the broader context of AGI research, such human-aligned evaluation may become increasingly important. As cognitive substrates evolve beyond probabilistic token predictors, naturalistic assessment of narrative quality, conceptual structure, and stylistic competence provides a more meaningful measure of emergent intelligence. GLPG’s evaluation method therefore represents a step toward future AGI benchmarks that reflect human linguistic judgement rather than purely statistical performance.

What is Scaling

GLPG supports architectural scaling: the hidden-layer capacity of the monolithic MLP can be increased or decreased while preserving the learned cognitive manifold. This behaviour is unusual among language models. Conventional architectures require full retraining when capacity changes, because their internal representations are statistically entangled. GLPG, however, separates structure (the deterministic grammar–lemma graph and paging geometry) from semantics (the learned lemma embeddings and MLP projection). As a result, the canonical world model can be preserved across architectural transformations.

Scaling therefore refers to the process of transforming a trained GLPG model into a larger or smaller version while retaining its attractor basins, canonical structure, and semantic geometry.

Conceptual Overview

GLPG scaling is governed by two principles:

  1. Structural invariance. The grammar–lemma graph, paging geometry, and learned lemma embeddings remain unchanged during scaling. These components form the cognitive DNA of the model.

  2. Semantic projection. The monolithic MLP is replaced by a larger or smaller network whose weights are derived from the original model through conservative embedding (upscaling) or projection (downscaling). This preserves the semantic manifold while adjusting its expressive capacity.

Because the canonical world model is preserved, scaling does not reset the model. Instead, the model continues training from a transformed but structurally identical cognitive substrate.

Upscaling

Upscaling increases the hidden-layer size of the monolithic MLP. The goal is to expand the semantic manifold without erasing existing attractors. GLPG accomplishes this by embedding the old weight matrices into the top-left corner of larger matrices and initializing all new parameters with extremely small values. This ensures that the expanded model behaves like the original model at the start of training, while providing additional capacity for semantic growth.

Formally, given an old hidden size H and a new size H′ > H, each linear layer is widened:

$$W' = \begin{pmatrix} W & \varepsilon \\ \varepsilon & \varepsilon \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b' = (b, \varepsilon),$$

where ε is tiny noise (e.g. 10−5). The old cognitive manifold is preserved in the upper-left block, while the new dimensions begin as neutral extensions of the existing space.

This mechanism allows a small, heavily trained model to seed a larger model that requires only light additional training to achieve high performance. Upscaling therefore acts as capacity-expansion without cognitive reset.

Downscaling

Downscaling reduces the hidden-layer size. The goal is to compress the semantic manifold into a smaller core while preserving its canonical structure. GLPG accomplishes this by projecting each large weight matrix onto its top-left block:

W′ = W[:H′,:H′],   b′ = b[:H′].

This operation extracts the most stable portion of the attractor manifold. Because GLPG’s semantic geometry is strongly shaped by paging and deterministic routing, the first H′ dimensions typically contain the core attractor basins. Downscaling therefore acts as cognitive compression.

Downscaled models often exhibit higher local coherence and stronger canonical pressure, even though their expressive capacity is reduced. This behaviour mirrors biological systems: smaller brains can retain stable behavioural patterns while losing higher-level variation.

Algorithmic Description

The upscaling and downscaling procedures are implemented directly in the GLPG reference engine. The following excerpt illustrates the core mechanism:

Upscaling: embed old weights into larger matrices; initialize new parameters with tiny noise.
Downscaling: project large matrices onto smaller blocks; retain the first H′ dimensions.

These operations preserve the functional behaviour of the model while adjusting its capacity. Training can continue immediately after scaling, allowing the model to refine its expanded or compressed manifold without losing previously learned structure.

Implications

Scaling reveals that GLPG behaves as a cognitive substrate rather than a statistical model. Its semantic manifold is stable, hereditary, and structurally grounded. Upscaling inflates the manifold; downscaling compresses it; staged training produces generational inheritance. The baseline model therefore serves not only as a reference point, but as the progenitor of an entire lineage of scaled models explored in the following sections.

What is Staged Training

Staged training is a multi-generational growth procedure in which a GLPG model is trained across several architectural capacities in sequence. Instead of training a single large model from scratch, the model is grown through a lineage of progressively larger hidden-layer sizes. Each stage inherits the canonical world model, lemma embeddings, and paging geometry from the previous stage, ensuring continuity of the attractor manifold.

This approach mirrors biological and cognitive development: a small system first consolidates its core attractors, after which larger systems expand and refine the inherited semantic manifold. Staged training therefore provides a controlled mechanism for cognitive growth.

Conceptual Overview

The example staged training pipeline consists of four phases:

  1. Stage 1: Smaller model (384 units). The model begins with low capacity, forcing strong attractor consolidation. This stage forms the “genetic core” of the lineage.

  2. Stage 2: Baseline-scale model (768 units). The model expands its semantic manifold while retaining the Stage 1 attractors. New attractor pockets emerge.

  3. Stage 3: Larger model (1152 units). The manifold becomes richer and more expressive. This stage often produces the most balanced cognitive behaviour.

  4. Stage 4: Largest model (1536 units). The model reaches full capacity. If trained sufficiently, this stage yields a mature attractor manifold.

Each stage begins from the scaled version of the previous stage rather than from random initialization. This ensures hereditary transfer of attractors and prevents cognitive reset.

Epoch Allocation

Total training epochs are distributed across stages using fixed multipliers:

Multipliers = [4, 3, 2, 1].

Given a total epoch budget E, each stage receives:

$$E_i = \left\lfloor \frac{E}{4 + 3 + 2 + 1} \right\rfloor \times \text{Multiplier}_i.$$

For example, with E = 200, 000 epochs as budget:

$$\begin{aligned} E_1 &= 80{,}000,\\ E_2 &= 60{,}000,\\ E_3 &= 40{,}000,\\ E_4 &= 20{,}000, \end{aligned}$$

yielding a total of 200,000 epochs.

This allocation reflects the cognitive principle that smaller models require more training to stabilize their attractor basins, while larger models require fewer epochs to refine inherited structure.

Algorithmic Description

Staged training is implemented in the GLPG engine through two operations:

  1. Scaling. The model is upscaled or downscaled to the next hidden size using the function-preserving transformations described in Section 5. The canonical world model remains unchanged.

  2. Training. The scaled model is trained for Ei randomized epochs using the same curriculum as the baseline model. This refines the inherited attractor manifold at the new capacity.

The following pseudocode summarizes the procedure:

for each stage i: scale model to hidden size Hi train model for Ei epochs save model as next-stage ancestor

This process yields a lineage of models, each inheriting and expanding the attractor geometry of its predecessor.

Rationale

Staged training exploits a key property of GLPG: its attractor manifold is stable under architectural scaling. A small model can be trained until its attractors are deeply consolidated. Upscaling then embeds this manifold into a larger semantic space, allowing new attractors to form without erasing the old ones. The result is a cognitively richer model that requires far fewer epochs than a large model trained from scratch.

This behaviour is analogous to biological development: early consolidation, followed by capacity expansion, followed by refinement. Staged training therefore provides a computational analogue of cognitive growth and inheritance within the GLPG architecture.

Experimental Setup

This section defines the canonical datasets, hyperparameters, and baseline model configuration used throughout all scaling experiments. The baseline model serves as the reference point against which upscaling, downscaling, staged training, and cross-dataset inheritance are evaluated.

Datasets

All experiments use canonical grammar–lemma corpora derived from two complete Gutenberg novels. These datasets provide compact yet structurally rich narrative biomes suitable for observing GLPG’s attractor dynamics. Both datasets exhibit regular syntactic cadence and constrained vocabulary, making them ideal substrates for studying GLPG’s semantic manifold formation and scaling behaviour.

A Honeymoon in Space

  • 357 KB raw text

  • 709 KB canonical grammar–lemma tokens

  • 54,039 unique token-transition samples

  • 685 sequences

The Time Machine

  • 180 KB raw text

  • 458 KB canonical grammar–lemma tokens

  • 35,642 unique token-transition samples

  • 238 sequences

Hyperparameters

All models share the same structural hyperparameters unless otherwise specified:

  • Input vector (124 dimensions): 44 CSL features (current token, sequence index, line index), 48 token-history features, 32 recurrent narrative-memory features

  • Output embedding: 128-dimensional learned lemma embedding

  • Baseline model hidden size: 768 units

  • Paging geometry: 1024 pages with a maximum of 8 lemma inputs per page (restricting the candidate token space and improving routing determinism)

These hyperparameters define the canonical GLPG manifold width used throughout the scaling experiments.

Transfer Learning Examples

Transfer learning in GLPG provides a direct window into the behaviour of its attractor manifold. Because GLPG is a canon‑closed architecture whose semantic geometry is shaped by deterministic paging and learned lemma embeddings, mixing datasets does not simply combine stylistic features. Instead, transfer learning reveals how strongly each dataset imprints its attractor basins onto the manifold, and how these basins reorganise when a second dataset is introduced.

A hybrid model always retains the combined vocabulary of both datasets, but the active canon—the semantic and structural behaviour expressed during generation—depends entirely on the relative strength of the attractor basins formed during training.

Transfer Learning Canon Dynamics

These experiments reveal a fundamental cognitive property of GLPG: transfer learning is canon‑dominant. The dataset with the strongest attractor structure determines the active canon of the hybrid model, regardless of the combined vocabulary.

Several dynamics emerge:

  • Asymmetric inheritance. The semantic manifold reorganises toward the second dataset. Training exclusively on domain B after domain A causes A to be largely overwritten by B.

  • Attractor dominance. The dataset with stronger syntactic cadence, more stable lemma clusters, and higher canon pressure dominates the manifold. In practice, A Honeymoon in Space exhibits stronger attractors than The Time Machine, but in both directions the second dataset fully determines the resulting canon.

  • Lemma persistence without semantics. Vocabulary from the first dataset remains present in embedding space, but without semantic activation. These tokens appear only as isolated artefacts, not as coherent narrative structures.

  • Cognitive overwriting. This behaviour mirrors biological cognitive systems: training a human or animal exclusively in domain B causes domain A to fade. Previously consolidated attractors weaken when new attractors are repeatedly reinforced.

  • Consolidation requires repetition. To maintain competence in both domains A and B, both must be trained repeatedly. Without continued exposure, one domain will dominate and overwrite the other.

Comparison with Conventional Language Models

Transformers exhibit a superficially similar phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting, driven by:

  • gradient overwriting,

  • loss‑driven collapse,

  • embedding drift,

  • attention‑pattern overwriting.

However, the mechanism in GLPG is fundamentally different. Transformers forget because new gradients overwrite statistical correlations. GLPG forgets because new attractor basins reorganise the semantic manifold in a deterministic, interpretable way.

Where transformers lose information unpredictably, GLPG exhibits:

  • predictable attractor dominance,

  • traceable manifold reshaping,

  • canon‑closed overwriting,

  • cognitively interpretable inheritance dynamics.

This makes GLPG’s transfer‑learning behaviour not a failure mode, but a hallmark of its identity as a minimal cognitive substrate.

Summary

Transfer learning in GLPG does not blend canons. It reveals how semantic attractors compete, reorganise, and dominate within a unified manifold. The second dataset consistently overwrites the first, unless both are trained repeatedly. This behaviour aligns with biological cognition and distinguishes GLPG from conventional language models, reinforcing its role as a structurally grounded and cognitively interpretable substrate.

Textbook Scaling Behaviour

Across all scaling experiments, GLPG exhibits a remarkably consistent and textbook-like developmental pattern. Upscaling monotonically improves semantic richness and attractor depth; downscaling compresses the manifold while preserving canonical structure; tiny models reach a clear capacity limit; lineage-based XL models outperform all baselines; and XL models trained from scratch collapse due to insufficient consolidation. These behaviours occur uniformly across all model sizes, generational paths, and datasets.

Beyond these monotonic scaling laws, GLPG displays an additional and highly unusual property: structural and semantic stability under variation. Across hyperparameter sweeps, scaling directions, staged learning regimes, transfer learning experiments, and dual-mode generation, the model’s attractor manifold remains coherent and predictable. Changes in learning rate, batch size, initialization, training depth, or model capacity do not produce chaotic jumps, collapses, or irregularities. Instead, GLPG consistently deforms its manifold in smooth, interpretable ways.

This stability is not typical for language models. Transformer-based architectures often show non-monotonic scaling behaviour, irregular performance jumps, catastrophic forgetting, embedding drift, and sensitivity to initialization and optimizer dynamics. Small changes in hyperparameters or dataset composition can produce large, unpredictable shifts in output quality. GLPG, by contrast, behaves as a low-dimensional cognitive dynamical system: consolidation at smaller capacities stabilizes the attractor manifold, which can then be expanded through upscaling without resetting the learned world model. Downscaling compresses the manifold without destroying canonical structure, and transfer learning reorganizes attractors in a deterministic, canon-dominant manner.

The textbook nature of GLPG’s scaling behaviour arises from its architectural separation between structure and semantics. The deterministic grammar–lemma graph and paging geometry provide a stable backbone, while the monolithic MLP acts as a semantic projector whose capacity can be increased or decreased without entangling the underlying canonical world. Function-preserving scaling therefore yields predictable cognitive deformation: compression, stabilization, expansion, and refinement.

This combination of monotonic scaling laws and cross-regime stability makes GLPG uniquely suitable for staged growth, lineage-based training, and controlled cognitive evolution. The scaling results in this paper demonstrate that GLPG’s manifold behaves not as a statistical correlation space, but as a structured cognitive substrate whose development follows clear, reproducible, and biologically-plausible patterns.

Relevance for AGI

GLPG exhibits a constellation of properties that are rare or entirely absent in conventional language model architectures. Most neural language models—MLPs, RNNs, LSTMs, and transformers—do not support function-preserving scaling, do not preserve their attractor manifold under architectural change, and do not maintain stable behaviour under hyperparameter variation, dataset mixing, or staged training. Increasing or decreasing model capacity in such systems typically requires full retraining, and the resulting model is not a continuation of the smaller model but a new statistical system with different inductive biases, different attractors, and different failure modes.

GLPG behaves differently. Its monolithic MLP supports function-preserving hidden-layer doubling: upscaling expands capacity while preserving the attractor manifold; downscaling compresses the manifold without destroying its structure. As a result, GLPG models can grow and shrink without cognitive reset. This behaviour is reinforced by a second, equally unusual property: structural and semantic stability. Across hyperparameter sweeps, scaling experiments, staged learning, transfer learning, and dual-mode generation, GLPG’s manifold deforms smoothly and predictably. No chaotic jumps, collapses, or irregularities appear. This stability is not typical for statistical language models and reflects GLPG’s identity as a low-dimensional cognitive dynamical system.

The experiments in this paper demonstrate that GLPG maintains a stable cognitive manifold under both upscaling and downscaling. Downscaling acts as a form of self-distillation. Instead of training a new student model on a new dataset, GLPG compresses its own manifold into a smaller semantic core. This core retains the essential attractors of the parent model. Subsequent upscaling expands this core into a larger semantic space, producing a model that is structurally similar to the parent but often more stable and more coherent.

upscaling = capacity expansion,   downscaling = self-distillation.

This is not catastrophic forgetting in the transformer sense. Graph-Based QKV Selection in GLPG

GLPG does not use attention, nor does it rely on the query–key–value mechanism found in transformers. However, the structure of the GLPG language graph naturally induces an analogous triplet. This analogy is not architectural but conceptual: it clarifies which parts of the GLPG pipeline are already deterministic, and which part remains an open challenge for further quality improvements.

Deterministic K and V in the Language Graph

In GLPG, every lemma is a node in the canonical language graph. Each node has a set of outgoing transitions representing all semantically valid continuations. This yields two deterministic components:

  • K (Key): the lemma itself, representing where we are in the canonical world. The key is not a learned vector but a structural position in the graph.

  • V (Value): the set of all outgoing transitions from that lemma, representing what can happen next. The value is not a dense projection but a deterministic transition set.

Thus, GLPG already provides the “K” and “V” components through its world model. They are not learned; they are part of the canonical structure.

Q as Context-Derived Motivation

The missing component is the Q (Query), which must determine why a particular transition from V(K) should be selected. Unlike transformers, where Q is a learned linear projection of the token embedding, GLPG must derive Q from the input vector and the paging context.

Q must encode:

  • attractor pressure,

  • narrative intent,

  • world-state consistency,

  • lemma-embedding similarity,

  • and the cognitive phase of the model.

At present, GLPG uses simple context encoders to approximate Q. These encoders work, but they are not yet expressive enough to fully exploit the structure of the graph. Improving Q is therefore one of the main challenges for future GLPG development.

Why This Analogy Matters

This QKV interpretation is not a transformer claim. It is a structural analogy that highlights the division of labour inside GLPG:

K = where,   Q = why,   V = what.

Transformers must learn all three components from scratch. GLPG already has K and V embedded in the canonical graph. Only Q must be inferred from context.

This means that GLPG is inherently closer to a semantic router than to an attention mechanism. The model does not search over all tokens; it selects among the transitions that the world model allows. Improving Q will therefore directly improve semantic precision, coherence, and narrative stability.

Open Challenge

The next major step for GLPG is to strengthen the context encoder so that Q becomes a reliable, high-resolution motivation signal. When Q is sufficiently expressive, GLPG will effectively implement a graph-based QKV selection mechanism:

V* = argmaxv ∈ V(K)compatibility(Q,v),

where compatibility is determined by attractor matching, canonical consistency, and paging context.

This is not attention. It is a deterministic world model guided by a learned motivational vector. But the structural analogy clarifies what is already solved (K and V) and what remains to be improved (Q).

Improving Q is therefore one of the key challenges for increasing GLPG output quality and for enabling more complex multi-agent and multi-world experiments such as the AGI School.
