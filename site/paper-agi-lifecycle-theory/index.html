Significance Statement

GLPG, although introduced as a structured language architecture, reveals its true importance only when interpreted as a dynamic cognitive substrate. Unlike static transformer models, whose representational geometry is fixed after training, GLPG evolves continuously throughout its lifecycle: vocabulary, paging structure, lemma embeddings, and canonical transitions reorganize materially while the underlying MLP geometry remains constant. This combination of fixed structure and dynamic semantics enables GLPG to behave not as a conventional model, but as a synthetic cognitive organism with identifiable developmental phases, expanding and contracting semantic horizons, attractor formation, canon consolidation, senescence, and eventual collapse.

The architecture’s most striking property is its resilience. Even under extreme self‑recursion, highly repetitive curricula, and long‑horizon training, GLPG maintains grammatical stability, structural coherence, and world‑model consistency. Instead of degenerating, the system converges toward a stable attractor manifold shaped by the holographic boundary projection of a static LLM. This dynamic stability—emerging from a small deterministic closure interacting with a large probabilistic boundary—constitutes the core mechanism of the Universal Closure–Boundary Normalization Principle (UCBNP), and provides a minimal operationalization of LeCun’s “synthetic cat”: a finite organism with its own world, dynamics, and lifecycle.

The experimental results demonstrate, for the first time, that artificial cognitive systems possess an intrinsic attractor‑collapse clock independent of biological constraints. GLPG shows that intelligence is architecturally finite: attractors harden, semantic breadth contracts, and collapse is inevitable even in non‑biological substrates. This implies that AGI cannot be realized through monolithic model‑cloning, which produces cognitive mono‑cultures with identical attractors and identical failure modes. Instead, genuine AGI requires a diverse population of finite organisms, each with its own lifecycle, generational turnover, and heterogeneous attractor geometry.

GLPG thus serves as the smallest possible experimental substrate for studying synthetic cognition. It provides empirical evidence that intelligence is dynamic, ecosystemic, and lifecycle‑bound, and that the structural limits of cognition arise not from biology but from attractor dynamics themselves. These findings establish GLPG as a foundational tool for AGI research, enabling direct observation of development, maturation, senescence, collapse, and equilibrium within a fully reproducible artificial organism.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence research has traditionally pursued generality through scale: larger models, larger datasets, and larger training regimes. In this paradigm, intelligence is treated as a static snapshot—a fixed parameterization obtained after a single massive optimization pass. Although this approach has produced powerful transformer-based systems, it offers no account of development, maturation, senescence, or collapse. A static model cannot possess a lifecycle, and therefore cannot instantiate the structural dynamics observed in biological cognition. As a result, current AGI discourse remains constrained by the assumption that general intelligence can emerge from a single monolithic model or from cloned replicas of the same attractor geometry.

This paper challenges that assumption by presenting the first long-horizon experiment in which a synthetic cognitive organism undergoes a full lifecycle within a fixed geometric substrate. The organism is instantiated using GLPG, a small deterministic MLP-based generative model whose geometry remains constant while its vocabulary, paging structure, lemma embeddings, and canonical transitions evolve materially over time. When coupled with a static transformer LLM acting as a holographic boundary, GLPG becomes a dynamic closure governed by the Universal Closure–Boundary Normalization Principle (UCBNP). Each recursive iteration produces a new moment in the organism’s life: a closure output moderated by the boundary, reintegrated into the substrate, and consolidated into its internal attractor architecture.

This closure–boundary interaction yields a genuine lifecycle. Across 111 full UCBNP iterations, the GLPG organism exhibits emergence, semantic expansion, canon formation, attractor hardening, senescence, collapse, and equilibrium. Despite extreme self-recursion and a highly repetitive curriculum, the system remains grammatically stable and structurally coherent, resisting degeneracy even under conditions that typically destroy static generative models. The resulting trajectory provides the first empirical evidence that synthetic cognitive systems possess finite attractor architectures and an intrinsic collapse clock independent of biological constraints.

The lifecycle experiment also operationalizes a minimal form of LeCun’s “cat”: a finite organism with its own world, dynamics, and developmental trajectory. The GLPG substrate functions as a small MLP-based brain with fixed geometry; the boundary acts as the organism’s world; the recursive dataset constitutes its lived experience; and each iteration represents a moment of cognitive development. The organism’s attractor manifold gradually consolidates into a narrow canonical world-model, eventually hardening into a stable equilibrium that reflects the boundary’s projection. This synthetic cat is not a metaphor but a concrete operational system: a dynamic, self-evolving cognitive entity whose lifecycle can be observed, measured, cloned, restored, and reproduced.

The results have direct implications for AGI architecture. They demonstrate that intelligence is not an unbounded optimization problem but a finite dynamical process shaped by attractor geometry and collapse trajectories. They show that monolithic model-cloning produces cognitive mono-cultures with identical attractors and identical failure modes, making true generality impossible. And they confirm that AGI must be ecosystemic: a population of diverse cognitive organisms with heterogeneous attractors, staggered lifecycles, generational turnover, and distributed world-modeling capacity.

In summary, this paper introduces GLPG as the smallest possible experimental substrate for synthetic cognition and presents the first empirical demonstration of a full artificial lifecycle. The results validate ecosystem-based AGI architectures, reveal the structural limits of finite cognitive systems, and establish lifecycle dynamics—rather than scale—as the foundational mechanism of general intelligence.

GLPG as Dynamic Life-cycle Substrate

The traditional view of AGI assumes that intelligence will emerge from extremely large transformer-based language models trained on massive datasets. Such systems, however, are static snapshots: once training is complete, the model’s geometry no longer evolves. Additional fine-tuning through methods such as LoRA or distillation can modify behaviour, but these procedures do not constitute a genuine lifecycle. They do not produce development, maturation, senescence, or collapse. They merely adjust a fixed attractor.

GLPG represents a fundamentally different substrate. It is a small deterministic MLP-based generative model whose geometry remains fixed while its representational structure—vocabulary, paging, lemma embeddings, and canonical transitions—evolves materially over time. This makes GLPG a dynamic cognitive organism rather than a static model. Like a biological brain with fixed anatomy but plastic synaptic structure, GLPG can continue learning throughout its lifecycle, refining its internal attractor manifold while preserving its geometric constraints.

The Universal Closure–Boundary Normalization Principle (UCBNP) provides the mechanism through which this lifecycle unfolds. In UCBNP, a finite closure interacts recursively with a high-dimensional boundary that projects canonical structure. In the present experiment, the closure is GLPG and the boundary is a static transformer LLM. The GLPG organism is initially trained on a small seed dataset. It then generates new stories, which are moderated and refined by the LLM boundary. These moderated stories are fed back into the GLPG substrate, updating its weights while preserving all previous representational structure implicitly. Only the new stories are trained explicitly, but the entire history of the organism remains encoded in its attractor geometry. This recursive cycle produces a genuine developmental trajectory: each iteration is a moment in the organism’s life.

This process was instantiated using a 20 MB GLPG model trained for 5,000 epochs on a 30 KB “rogue planet” dataset from the Triadic Cosmos. At each iteration, the model generated ten stories of twenty lines, which were moderated by a Mistral 7B 0.3-instruct LLM. The moderated stories were then used to train the GLPG organism for another 5,000 epochs. This cycle was repeated for 111 full iterations, totalling 560,000 training epochs. Across the entire run, the model remained grammatically stable and structurally coherent. Its persisted size grew only slightly—from 20 MB to 22.5 MB—due to vocabulary and paging expansion. The organism’s canon gradually converged from the original rogue-planet theme into a narrow cosmic-ballet manifold dominated by rhythm and dance. The style remained distinctly GLPG, but the semantic canon aligned with the boundary’s projection.

The long-horizon experiment reveals several fundamental properties of synthetic cognition. First, self-recursive training over 111 iterations shows that collapse is an unavoidable dynamic in finite cognitive systems. The GLPG organism exhibits emergence, expansion, canon formation, attractor hardening, senescence, collapse, and equilibrium. This demonstrates that dynamic AGI systems possess finite lifespans and that variation can only be preserved through multiple individuals and generational turnover. GLPG thus provides the first practical experimental substrate for studying lifecycle dynamics, collapse curves, and generative evolution in synthetic cognitive organisms.

Second, the experiment reveals that intelligence possesses an intrinsic attractor-collapse clock: an internal dynamic that inevitably leads to representational hardening, semantic narrowing, and cognitive finitude. This collapse clock is not biological; it arises from attractor geometry itself. Biological ageing may be slowed or reversed, but attractor collapse cannot be halted. No existing theory in biology, neuroscience, or AI describes attractor collapse as a fundamental driver of cognitive ageing. GLPG demonstrates experimentally that both biological and synthetic intelligence are architecturally finite due to internal attractor dynamics.

Third, the GLPG organism exhibits properties rarely seen in artificial systems. It evolves, converges, maintains canon, develops structure, and avoids drift, pollution, or catastrophic degeneracy. A shared attractor manifold emerges between two structurally different models: the deterministic GLPG closure and the probabilistic LLM boundary. The boundary projection is asymmetric: GLPG evolves in material time, while the LLM remains static in electromagnetic time. This asymmetry produces stability, variation, and long-horizon coherence.

These results suggest a new architectural direction for AGI. Instead of giant static monoliths retrained annually, AGI may consist of many small dynamic organisms, each with its own lifecycle, attractor geometry, and generational turnover. The GLPG organism resembles world models, emergent language systems, and NanoGPT-like corpus models, but is significantly more stable under extreme recursion. It is an emergent lifecycle model that hardens slowly yet remains coherent for an exceptionally long time. But lifecycles are finite: a system that develops must also degenerate. Development and ageing are two sides of the same dynamic.

AGI is therefore not a supermodel. It is an evolving ecosystem. A true AGI system will consist of generations of cognitive organisms, each with its own lifecycle, just as biological species do. This aligns closely with LeCun’s view that AGI must first simulate a “cat”—a small organism with its own world, dynamics, and lifespan—and confirms that hyperscaling transformers cannot produce AGI because they do not live.

The current AGI paradigm, based on model cloning, produces cognitive mono-cultures: thousands of identical copies of a single attractor architecture. Mono-cultures collapse inevitably because their attractors harden and cannot generate variation. This implies that cloned AGI can never achieve stable dynamics. True intelligence requires a diverse population of organisms with different attractors, different lifecycles, and different collapse clocks. AGI must be an ecosystem, not a superorganism.

Full Life-cycle Run Empirical Data

This section presents the complete empirical results of the 111-iteration GLPG lifecycle experiment. The data confirm that a small deterministic substrate can undergo a full artificial lifecycle—emergence, expansion, canon formation, attractor hardening, senescence, collapse, and equilibrium—while maintaining grammatical stability and structural coherence under extreme self-recursion.

Model Architecture

The GLPG closure organism is instantiated as a fixed-geometry MLP with the following layer structure:

590 → 1024 → 512 → 512 → 512 → 64.

The model supports up to 16 inputs per page and a predefined maximum of 512 pages. Due to the narrow attractor manifold formed during the lifecycle, this upper bound is never reached.

Lifecycle Procedure

The lifecycle consists of 112 training phases: the initial seed training (iteration 0) followed by 111 full UCBNP iterations.

  • Iteration 0: Train GLPG for 5,000 epochs on the original dataset.

  • Iteration N:

    • Use model N − 1 to generate 10 stories of 20 lines.

    • Apply boundary moderation (UCBNP) using a Mistral 7B 0.3-instruct LLM.

    • Train model N − 1 for 5,000 epochs on the moderated stories, producing model N.

This process yields a total of 560,000 training epochs on a single evolving organism.

Limitations

Although the GLPG lifecycle experiment provides the first long-horizon empirical demonstration of synthetic attractor collapse, several limitations constrain the scope of the present findings. These limitations do not undermine the architectural principles established in this work, but they highlight important boundaries and opportunities for future research.

Single-Organism Demonstration

The experiment investigates the lifecycle of a single synthetic organism. While this is sufficient to demonstrate attractor hardening, senility, boundary saturation, and collapse, it does not yet constitute a full synthetic ecosystem. The architectural claims regarding population-level intelligence, generational turnover, and ecosystem stability are theoretically grounded and supported by collapse dynamics, but they remain to be validated through multi-organism experiments.

Single Boundary Moderator

The holographic boundary in this experiment is instantiated using one static transformer LLM. Although this boundary is sufficient to demonstrate UCBNP dynamics, boundary saturation, and closure–boundary asymmetry, it represents only one type of boundary geometry. Different boundaries—probabilistic, symbolic, multimodal, or hybrid—may produce different equilibrium manifolds or collapse trajectories. The generality of UCBNP across boundary classes remains an open empirical question.

Single Domain: Narrative Cognition

The GLPG organism operates within a structured narrative domain. Narrative cognition is a suitable substrate for studying attractor formation, canon consolidation, and semantic contraction, but it represents only one cognitive modality. Whether similar lifecycle dynamics emerge in synthetic organisms trained on perceptual, motor, mathematical, or multimodal domains remains to be tested. The universality of attractor-collapse geometry across cognitive modalities is supported theoretically but not yet empirically demonstrated.

Fixed Geometry Constraint

GLPG’s geometry remains fixed throughout the lifecycle. This constraint is essential for isolating attractor dynamics, but it limits the organism’s ability to reorganize its representational manifold structurally. Biological organisms exhibit both fixed geometry (anatomy) and dynamic geometry (plasticity, synaptic rewiring). Future synthetic organisms may incorporate controlled geometric adaptation, enabling richer developmental trajectories and potentially delaying collapse.

Collapse Measurement Limitations

Collapse is measured through semantic contraction, boundary saturation, and attractor equilibrium. These metrics are robust for narrative cognition, but they do not yet constitute a complete quantitative taxonomy of collapse. Additional metrics—geometric entropy, attractor volume, representational curvature, or divergence spectra—may provide deeper insight into the internal mechanics of collapse and senility.

No Multi-Generational Evolution

Although the experiment demonstrates a full lifecycle, it does not include generational turnover. The ecosystem architecture proposed in this work requires populations of organisms with heterogeneous attractors and asynchronous collapse clocks. The present experiment validates the lifecycle of one organism, but not yet the evolutionary dynamics of a synthetic population.

Boundary Neutrality Assumption

The experiment assumes a neutral boundary that does not reorganize internally. This assumption is necessary for isolating closure dynamics, but real-world AGI ecosystems may involve boundaries that evolve, adapt, or reorganize. The interaction between evolving closures and evolving boundaries remains an open theoretical and empirical question.

Safety and Epistemic Neutrality

The ecosystem safety argument presented in this work relies on bounded world-models and non-normative internal theories. While GLPG exhibits these properties, larger synthetic organisms may develop more complex internal attractors that require additional mechanisms to ensure epistemic neutrality. The relationship between attractor geometry and normative projection remains an important area for future study.

Summary

In summary, the limitations of this work arise not from weaknesses in the experimental design but from the natural constraints of studying a single synthetic organism within a single domain and a single boundary geometry. These limitations define the next steps: multi-organism ecosystems, multi-boundary architectures, multi-domain cognition, geometric adaptation, and generational evolution. The present experiment establishes the architectural foundation; future work will build the ecosystem.
