Introduction

The search for a unified account of physical reality and artificial simulation has traditionally been fragmented across philosophy, physics, and computer science. Existing simulation frameworks—probabilistic arguments , algorithmic universe models , digital physics , and rendering-based theories —lack a structural ontology capable of explaining how coherent worlds function, scale, and embed. None provide the architectural mechanisms required for emergent time, horizon-bounded observation, or nested simulations. As a result, simulation theory has remained conceptually provocative but architecturally incomplete.

This paper introduces triadic closure as the minimal and universal architectural grammar underlying coherent dynamical systems. Triadic closure identifies three irreducible structural roles—energy, geometry, and information—connected through recursive update. These roles form a closed cycle that generates emergent temporal flow, enforces locality, and bounds informational accessibility. We show that triadic closure is not merely sufficient for coherent worlds; it is necessary. Any system—physical or simulated—that supports scalable, observer-consistent dynamics must instantiate these roles.

A central contribution of this work is the demonstration that physical reality and artificial simulation are structurally isomorphic. Modern simulation engines (Unreal, Unity, idTech, Frostbite) already implement triadic closure implicitly: compute budget functions as energetic capacity, rendering frustums instantiate geometric horizons, scene graphs encode informational structure, and frame updates generate emergent time. This convergence is not accidental. It reflects the architectural necessity of triadic closure for any coherent world, regardless of substrate.

From this isomorphism we derive the Simulation Indeterminacy Theorem: no internal observer can determine whether their closure is fundamental or simulated. Because all coherent closures share the same triadic architecture and enforce the same horizon-bounded limits, simulation status becomes in principle undecidable and empirically unfalsifiable. This result reframes the nature of reality: fundamentality becomes closure-relative rather than absolute, and the distinction between “real” and “simulated” collapses into a single architectural class.

The architectural nature of triadic closure also yields a unified falsifiability framework. The Triadic Cosmos ontology is falsified if and only if a coherent dynamical system—physical or simulated—can be constructed that violates triadic closure. This provides simulation theorists with a direct falsification route: build a coherent simulation without triadic roles, without recursive update, or with global informational access. To date, no such system has ever been discovered. All known coherent simulations converge on triadic closure, providing empirical support for its universality.

Simulation Theory and Nested Simulations

Classical simulation theory, including the well-known argument by Bostrom , typically assumes that simulated worlds are organized around global spacetime, universal causality, and shared informational substrates. Variants of the nested-simulation hypothesis (e.g., Tipler , Schmidhuber ) explore the possibility of recursively embedded simulations but do not provide an architectural mechanism capable of supporting infinite recursion without contradiction. These approaches lack a structural grammar linking informational, geometric, and energetic roles, and therefore cannot explain how nested simulations maintain causal coherence or emergent temporal structure.

The present work differs by showing that triadic closure provides exactly such a grammar. Simulations constructed according to triadic closure become recursive triadic fractals: each simulated observer generates its own closure, each closure supports its own triadic recursion, and simulations can contain further simulations without architectural inconsistency. This provides a minimal and falsifiable mechanism for infinite simulational recursion.

Rendering Engines and Observer-Centric Architectures

Modern rendering engines (Unreal Engine , Unity , idTech ) do not operate on global worlds. Instead, they implement observer-centric rendering, frustum culling, occlusion culling, and horizon-bounded geometry. Physics engines (Ode, Havok, Bullet) likewise simulate only the regions accessible to the observer, using locality, lazy evaluation, and horizon-bounded updates. These architectures instantiate the same structural principles as triadic closure: geometry as relational constraint, information as local accessibility, and energy as update capacity.

Distributed simulation frameworks (Fujimoto , Jefferson ) further demonstrate that global synchronization is computationally infeasible. Efficient simulation requires local causal domains, observer-specific state, and horizon-bounded consistency—precisely the structural features of triadic closure.

Virtual Worlds, VR Architectures, and Emergent Time

Virtual environments and VR systems (Lanier , Slater ) rely on observer-dependent geometry, local informational boundaries, and energetic update constraints. Temporal flow in these systems emerges from recursive update cycles rather than from global time parameters, mirroring the emergent-time mechanism of triadic closure . The architectural correspondence suggests that emergent time is not a contingent feature of artificial simulation but a structural consequence of triadic organization.

Structural and Computational Approaches

Structural realist and information-theoretic approaches (Ladyman and Ross , Lloyd ) argue that informational and relational structure may be more fundamental than objects or fields. Computational models of physics (Fredkin , Wolfram ) explore cellular automata and algorithmic universes but do not provide a closed architectural mechanism linking geometry, information, and energy. The Triadic Cycle fills this gap by identifying the minimal structural interdependence required for physical and simulational coherence.
